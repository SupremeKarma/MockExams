### Question 1
Evaluate:
$$\int_0^a \int_0^b (x^2 + y^2) \, dx \, dy$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Double Iterated Integral:** In the Cartesian plane, evaluate the inner integral first while holding the outer variable constant:
   $$\int_{y=0}^a \left[ \int_{x=0}^b (x^2 + y^2) \, dx \right] dy$$
2. **Integration Power Rule:**
   $$\int x^n \, dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C \quad (n \ne -1), \qquad \int k \, dx = kx + C$$
3. **Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:**
   $$\int_A^B f(x) \, dx = [F(x)]_A^B = F(B) - F(A)$$

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Identify the integral limits and order**
The inner integral is with respect to $x$ (from $0$ to $b$), and the outer integral is with respect to $y$ (from $0$ to $a$):
$$I = \int_{y=0}^a \left( \int_{x=0}^b (x^2 + y^2) \, dx \right) dy$$

**Step 2: Evaluate the inner integral with respect to $x$**
Treat $y$ as a constant:
$$\int_0^b (x^2 + y^2) \, dx = \left[ \frac{x^3}{3} + y^2 x \right]_{x=0}^{x=b}$$

Substitute the upper limit $x = b$ and lower limit $x = 0$:
$$= \left( \frac{b^3}{3} + y^2 b \right) - \left( \frac{0^3}{3} + y^2(0) \right)$$
$$= \frac{b^3}{3} + b y^2$$

**Step 3: Evaluate the outer integral with respect to $y$**
Substitute the result of Step 2 into the outer integral:
$$I = \int_0^a \left( \frac{b^3}{3} + b y^2 \right) \, dy$$

Integrate each term with respect to $y$:
$$I = \left[ \frac{b^3}{3} y + b \frac{y^3}{3} \right]_{y=0}^{y=a}$$

Substitute upper limit $y = a$ and lower limit $y = 0$:
$$I = \left( \frac{b^3 a}{3} + \frac{b a^3}{3} \right) - (0 + 0)$$
$$I = \frac{a b^3 + a^3 b}{3} = \frac{ab(a^2 + b^2)}{3}$$

---

#### ✅ Final Answer
$$\int_0^a \int_0^b (x^2 + y^2) \, dx \, dy = \frac{ab(a^2 + b^2)}{3}$$

> ⚠️ **Beginner Tip:** When integrating $y^2$ with respect to $x$, do NOT write $\frac{y^3}{3}$. Since $y$ is constant during the $x$-integration, $\int y^2 dx = y^2 \int 1 dx = y^2 x$.

### Question 2
Evaluate:
$$\int_0^1 \int_0^2 \int_0^3 xyz \, dx \, dy \, dz$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Separability of Multiple Integrals:** When the integrand factors completely as $f(x, y, z) = g(x) \cdot h(y) \cdot k(z)$ and all limits are constants, the triple integral factors into the product of three independent 1D integrals:
   $$\int_{z_1}^{z_2} \int_{y_1}^{y_2} \int_{x_1}^{x_2} g(x) h(y) k(z) \, dx \, dy \, dz = \left( \int_{x_1}^{x_2} g(x) \, dx \right) \left( \int_{y_1}^{y_2} h(y) \, dy \right) \left( \int_{z_1}^{z_2} k(z) \, dz \right)$$

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Check separability**
- Integrand: $f(x, y, z) = x \cdot y \cdot z$
- Limits: $x \in [0, 3]$, $y \in [0, 2]$, $z \in [0, 1]$ (all independent constants)

Therefore:
$$I = \left( \int_0^3 x \, dx \right) \cdot \left( \int_0^2 y \, dy \right) \cdot \left( \int_0^1 z \, dz \right)$$

**Step 2: Evaluate each single integral independently**
1. **$x$-integral:**
   $$\int_0^3 x \, dx = \left[ \frac{x^2}{2} \right]_0^3 = \frac{3^2}{2} - \frac{0^2}{2} = \frac{9}{2}$$
2. **$y$-integral:**
   $$\int_0^2 y \, dy = \left[ \frac{y^2}{2} \right]_0^2 = \frac{2^2}{2} - \frac{0^2}{2} = \frac{4}{2} = 2$$
3. **$z$-integral:**
   $$\int_0^1 z \, dz = \left[ \frac{z^2}{2} \right]_0^1 = \frac{1^2}{2} - \frac{0^2}{2} = \frac{1}{2}$$

**Step 3: Multiply the three scalar results**
$$I = \left( \frac{9}{2} \right) \times (2) \times \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)$$
$$I = \frac{9 \times 2 \times 1}{2 \times 1 \times 2} = \frac{18}{4} = \frac{9}{2} = 4.5$$

---

#### ✅ Final Answer
$$\int_0^1 \int_0^2 \int_0^3 xyz \, dx \, dy \, dz = \frac{9}{2} = 4.5$$

### Question 3
Solve:
$$\sqrt{1 - x^2} \, dy + \sqrt{1 - y^2} \, dx = 0$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Variable Separable Method:** Rearrange so all $y$-terms are with $dy$ and all $x$-terms are with $dx$.
2. **Standard Inverse Trigonometric Integral:**
   $$\int \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - u^2}} \, du = \sin^{-1} u + C$$
3. **Trigonometric Addition Formula:**
   $$\sin(A + B) = \sin A \cos B + \cos A \sin B$$
   If $\sin A = x \implies \cos A = \sqrt{1 - x^2}$, and $\sin B = y \implies \cos B = \sqrt{1 - y^2}$, then:
   $$\sin(A + B) = x \sqrt{1 - y^2} + y \sqrt{1 - x^2}$$

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Separate the variables**
Given:
$$\sqrt{1 - x^2} \, dy + \sqrt{1 - y^2} \, dx = 0$$

Divide throughout by $\sqrt{1 - x^2} \sqrt{1 - y^2}$:
$$\frac{dy}{\sqrt{1 - y^2}} + \frac{dx}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}} = 0$$

**Step 2: Integrate both sides**
$$\int \frac{dy}{\sqrt{1 - y^2}} + \int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}} = C_1$$
$$\sin^{-1} y + \sin^{-1} x = C_1$$

**Step 3: Simplify to algebraic form**
Take the sine of both sides:
$$\sin(\sin^{-1} x + \sin^{-1} y) = \sin C_1$$

Using $\sin(A + B) = \sin A \cos B + \cos A \sin B$ with $A = \sin^{-1} x$ and $B = \sin^{-1} y$:
$$x \sqrt{1 - y^2} + y \sqrt{1 - x^2} = c \quad (\text{where } c = \sin C_1)$$

---

#### ✅ Final Answer
$$x \sqrt{1 - y^2} + y \sqrt{1 - x^2} = c$$

### Question 4
Solve:
$$P^2 - y^2 = 0 \quad \text{where } P = \frac{dy}{dx}$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Equations Solvable for $P$:** When a first-order differential equation of higher degree can be factored into linear factors in $P = \frac{dy}{dx}$:
   $$(P - f_1(x, y))(P - f_2(x, y)) = 0$$
   Each factor gives an independent first-order ODE.
2. **Combined Solution:** If the solutions to the factors are $g_1(x, y, c) = 0$ and $g_2(x, y, c) = 0$, the general solution is $g_1(x, y, c) \cdot g_2(x, y, c) = 0$.

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Factorize the quadratic in $P$**
$$P^2 - y^2 = 0 \implies (P - y)(P + y) = 0$$

This gives two separate first-order differential equations:
1. $P - y = 0 \implies \frac{dy}{dx} - y = 0$
2. $P + y = 0 \implies \frac{dy}{dx} + y = 0$

**Step 2: Solve the first equation $\frac{dy}{dx} = y$**
$$\frac{dy}{y} = dx \implies \int \frac{dy}{y} = \int dx \implies \ln|y| = x + \ln c_1 \implies y = c_1 e^x \implies y e^{-x} - c = 0$$

**Step 3: Solve the second equation $\frac{dy}{dx} = -y$**
$$\frac{dy}{y} = -dx \implies \int \frac{dy}{y} = -\int dx \implies \ln|y| = -x + \ln c_2 \implies y = c_2 e^{-x} \implies y e^x - c = 0$$

**Step 4: Combine into a single general solution**
$$(y - c e^x)(y - c e^{-x}) = 0$$

---

#### ✅ Final Answer
$$(y - c e^x)(y - c e^{-x}) = 0$$

### Question 8
Show that $f(z) = |z|^2$ is continuous everywhere in the complex plane.

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Continuity in $\mathbb{C}$:** A function $f(z) = u(x, y) + iv(x, y)$ is continuous at $z_0 = (x_0, y_0)$ if and only if both real functions $u(x, y)$ and $v(x, y)$ are continuous at $(x_0, y_0)$.
2. Polynomials in real variables $x$ and $y$ are continuous everywhere on $\mathbb{R}^2$.

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Express $|z|^2$ in Cartesian form**
Let $z = x + iy$.
$$|z| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \implies |z|^2 = x^2 + y^2$$

Thus:
$$f(z) = u(x, y) + i v(x, y)$$
where:
$$u(x, y) = x^2 + y^2, \qquad v(x, y) = 0$$

**Step 2: Check continuity of real and imaginary parts**
- $u(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$ is a polynomial in two real variables $x$ and $y$. By fundamental calculus theorems, all polynomials are continuous everywhere in $\mathbb{R}^2$.
- $v(x, y) = 0$ is a constant function, which is continuous everywhere.

**Step 3: Conclusion**
Since both $u(x, y)$ and $v(x, y)$ are continuous at every point $(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2$, the complex function $f(z) = |z|^2$ is continuous everywhere in $\mathbb{C}$.

### Question 10
Find the order of the pole and the residue of the function:
$$f(z) = \frac{z + 1}{z(z - 2)} \quad \text{at } z = 0.$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. Pole order from denominator factors.
2. Residue formula for simple pole: $\text{Res}(f, z_0) = \lim_{z \to z_0} (z - z_0) f(z)$.

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Determine the pole order**
The denominator has a single factor $z^1$, and numerator at $z = 0$ is $0 + 1 = 1 \ne 0$.
Hence $z = 0$ is a pole of order 1 (**simple pole**).

**Step 2: Compute the residue**

---

#### ✅ Final Answer
- Order of pole: **1 (Simple pole)**
- Residue: $\mathbf{-\frac{1}{2}}$

### Question 11
Find the Fourier integral of the function:
$$f(x) = \begin{cases} x, & \text{for } |x| < 1 \\ 0, & \text{for } |x| > 1 \end{cases}$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Fourier Sine Integral for Odd Function:** Since $f(-x) = -f(x)$, $f(x)$ is odd:
   $$A(\lambda) = 0$$
   $$B(\lambda) = 2 \int_0^\infty f(t) \sin(\lambda t) \, dt$$
   $$f(x) = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_0^\infty B(\lambda) \sin(\lambda x) \, d\lambda = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^\infty \left( \int_0^1 t \sin(\lambda t) \, dt \right) \sin(\lambda x) \, d\lambda$$

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Evaluate $B(\lambda)$**
$$B(\lambda) = 2 \int_0^1 t \sin(\lambda t) \, dt$$

Integrate by parts with $u = t \implies u' = 1$, $v = \sin(\lambda t) \implies \int v = -\frac{\cos(\lambda t)}{\lambda}$:
$$\int_0^1 t \sin(\lambda t) \, dt = \left[ -t \frac{\cos(\lambda t)}{\lambda} \right]_0^1 - \int_0^1 \left(-\frac{\cos(\lambda t)}{\lambda}\right) dt$$
$$= \left( -\frac{\cos\lambda}{\lambda} - 0 \right) + \left[ \frac{\sin(\lambda t)}{\lambda^2} \right]_0^1 = -\frac{\cos\lambda}{\lambda} + \frac{\sin\lambda}{\lambda^2} = \frac{\sin\lambda - \lambda\cos\lambda}{\lambda^2}$$

Multiply by 2:
$$B(\lambda) = 2 \left( \frac{\sin\lambda - \lambda\cos\lambda}{\lambda^2} \right)$$

**Step 2: Form the Fourier Integral**
$$f(x) = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_0^\infty B(\lambda) \sin(\lambda x) \, d\lambda$$
$$f(x) = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^\infty \left( \frac{\sin\lambda - \lambda\cos\lambda}{\lambda^2} \right) \sin(\lambda x) \, d\lambda$$

### Question 15
Expand the function $f(x) = \sin x$ in a Fourier cosine series in the interval $0 < x < \pi$, and deduce that:
$$\frac{1}{1 \cdot 3} + \frac{1}{3 \cdot 5} + \frac{1}{5 \cdot 7} + \dots = \frac{1}{2}$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. Cosine series on $(0, \pi)$:
   $$a_0 = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^\pi \sin x \, dx = \frac{4}{\pi} \implies \frac{a_0}{2} = \frac{2}{\pi}$$
2. $a_n = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^\pi \sin x \cos(nx) \, dx = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_0^\pi [\sin(n+1)x - \sin(n-1)x] dx$:
   - For odd $n$: $a_n = 0$.
   - For even $n = 2m$: $a_{2m} = -\frac{4}{\pi(4m^2 - 1)}$.

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Compute $a_0$**
$$a_0 = \frac{2}{\pi} [-\cos x]_0^\pi = \frac{2}{\pi}( -(-1) - (-1) ) = \frac{4}{\pi} \implies \frac{a_0}{2} = \frac{2}{\pi}$$

**Step 2: Compute $a_n$**
Using product-to-sum identity $2\sin x \cos(nx) = \sin(n+1)x - \sin(n-1)x$:
$$a_n = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_0^\pi [\sin(n+1)x - \sin(n-1)x] \, dx$$
For $n \ne 1$:
$$= \frac{1}{\pi} \left[ -\frac{\cos(n+1)x}{n+1} + \frac{\cos(n-1)x}{n-1} \right]_0^\pi$$
When $n$ is odd, $n+1$ and $n-1$ are even $\implies \cos(n\pm 1)\pi = 1 \implies a_n = 0$.
When $n$ is even ($n = 2m$):
$$a_{2m} = -\frac{4}{\pi(4m^2 - 1)}$$

**Step 3: Form the cosine series**
$$\sin x = \frac{2}{\pi} - \frac{4}{\pi} \sum_{m=1}^\infty \frac{\cos(2mx)}{4m^2 - 1}$$

**Step 4: Deduction at $x = 0$**
Put $x = 0$ (where $\sin 0 = 0$ and $\cos(0) = 1$):
$$0 = \frac{2}{\pi} - \frac{4}{\pi} \sum_{m=1}^\infty \frac{1}{4m^2 - 1}$$
$$\frac{4}{\pi} \sum_{m=1}^\infty \frac{1}{4m^2 - 1} = \frac{2}{\pi} \implies \sum_{m=1}^\infty \frac{1}{4m^2 - 1} = \frac{2}{4} = \frac{1}{2}$$
$$\frac{1}{1 \cdot 3} + \frac{1}{3 \cdot 5} + \frac{1}{5 \cdot 7} + \dots = \frac{1}{2}$$

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

### Question 25
Find the residue of $f(z) = \frac{3z - 4}{z(z - 1)(z - 2)}$ at each of its poles.

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. Simple poles at $z = 0, 1, 2$.
2. $\text{Res}(f, z_0) = \lim_{z \to z_0} (z - z_0) f(z)$.

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Residue at $z = 0$**
$$\text{Res}(f, 0) = \lim_{z \to 0} \frac{3z - 4}{(z - 1)(z - 2)} = \frac{-4}{(-1)(-2)} = \frac{-4}{2} = -2$$

**Step 2: Residue at $z = 1$**
$$\text{Res}(f, 1) = \lim_{z \to 1} \frac{3z - 4}{z(z - 2)} = \frac{3(1) - 4}{1(1 - 2)} = \frac{-1}{-1} = 1$$

**Step 3: Residue at $z = 2$**
$$\text{Res}(f, 2) = \lim_{z \to 2} \frac{3z - 4}{z(z - 1)} = \frac{3(2) - 4}{2(2 - 1)} = \frac{2}{2} = 1$$

**Check:** $\sum \text{Res} = -2 + 1 + 1 = 0 \quad \checkmark$

---

#### ✅ Final Answer
- $\text{Res}(f, 0) = \mathbf{-2}$
- $\text{Res}(f, 1) = \mathbf{1}$
- $\text{Res}(f, 2) = \mathbf{1}$
