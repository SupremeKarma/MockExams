### Question 1
Evaluate the double integral:
$$\int_0^2 \int_0^1 (x^2 + y^2) \, dx \, dy$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Iterated Double Integral:** Evaluated from the inside out:
   $$\int_{y=c}^d \left[ \int_{x=a}^b f(x, y) \, dx \right] dy$$
2. **Partial Integration Rule:** When integrating with respect to $x$, treat $y$ as a constant.
3. **Power Rule:** $\int x^n \, dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C$.

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Evaluate the inner integral with respect to $x$**
Treat $y$ as a constant:
$$\int_0^1 (x^2 + y^2) \, dx = \left[ \frac{x^3}{3} + y^2 x \right]_0^1$$
Substitute upper and lower limits for $x$:
$$= \left( \frac{1^3}{3} + y^2(1) \right) - \left( 0 + 0 \right) = \frac{1}{3} + y^2$$

**Step 2: Evaluate the outer integral with respect to $y$**
$$\int_0^2 \left( \frac{1}{3} + y^2 \right) dy = \left[ \frac{y}{3} + \frac{y^3}{3} \right]_0^2$$
Substitute upper limit $y = 2$ and lower limit $y = 0$:
$$= \left( \frac{2}{3} + \frac{2^3}{3} \right) - 0 = \frac{2}{3} + \frac{8}{3} = \frac{10}{3}$$

**Final Answer:**
$$\int_0^2 \int_0^1 (x^2 + y^2) \, dx \, dy = \frac{10}{3}$$

### Question 2
Define Taylor's theorem and Laurent's series.

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
Both theorems describe series representations of complex functions $f(z)$ in terms of powers of $(z - z_0)$.

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Taylor's Theorem (Complex Analytic Functions)**
- **Statement:** If $f(z)$ is analytic everywhere inside an open disk $D: |z - z_0| < R$, then $f(z)$ can be expanded uniquely as a power series:
  $$f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n (z - z_0)^n = a_0 + a_1(z - z_0) + a_2(z - z_0)^2 + \dots$$
- **Coefficients:** The coefficients are given by:
  $$a_n = \frac{f^{(n)}(z_0)}{n!} = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_C \frac{f(w)}{(w - z_0)^{n+1}} \, dw$$
  where $C$ is any simple closed contour inside $D$ enclosing $z_0$.

**Step 2: Laurent's Series**
- **Statement:** If $f(z)$ is analytic in an annular domain $D: r < |z - z_0| < R$, then $f(z)$ can be expanded in positive and negative integer powers of $(z - z_0)$:
  $$f(z) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty a_n (z - z_0)^n = \underbrace{\sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n (z - z_0)^n}_{\text{Analytic Part}} + \underbrace{\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{b_n}{(z - z_0)^n}}_{\text{Principal Part}}$$
- **Coefficients:** For any contour $C$ lying inside the annulus:
  $$a_n = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_C \frac{f(w)}{(w - z_0)^{n+1}} \, dw, \quad n \in \mathbb{Z}$$

### Question 3
Determine the order and degree of the differential equation:
$$\sqrt{\frac{d^3 y}{dx^3}} = \frac{dy}{dx}$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Order:** The highest derivative present in the differential equation.
2. **Degree:** The highest power (exponent) of the highest-order derivative after clearing all fractional powers and radicals involving derivatives.

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Clear the radical**
Square both sides of the equation:
$$\left( \sqrt{\frac{d^3 y}{dx^3}} \right)^2 = \left( \frac{dy}{dx} \right)^2$$
$$\frac{d^3 y}{dx^3} = \left( \frac{dy}{dx} \right)^2$$

**Step 2: Determine Order and Degree**
- The highest derivative occurring in the equation is $\frac{d^3 y}{dx^3}$ (the third derivative). Hence:
  $$\text{Order} = 3$$
- The exponent of this highest derivative $\left(\frac{d^3 y}{dx^3}\right)^1$ is $1$. Hence:
  $$\text{Degree} = 1$$

### Question 4
Solve:
$$(x + 1) \, dy + (y - 1) \, dx = 0$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Separation of Variables:** Rearrange the equation into the form $g(y) \, dy + f(x) \, dx = 0$ and integrate both sides directly.

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Separate the variables**
Divide both sides by $(x + 1)(y - 1)$ (assuming $x \ne -1, y \ne 1$):
$$\frac{dy}{y - 1} + \frac{dx}{x + 1} = 0$$

**Step 2: Integrate both sides**
$$\int \frac{dy}{y - 1} + \int \frac{dx}{x + 1} = \ln C$$
$$\ln|y - 1| + \ln|x + 1| = \ln C$$

**Step 3: Combine logarithms and exponentiate**
$$\ln|(x + 1)(y - 1)| = \ln C$$
$$(x + 1)(y - 1) = C$$

**Final Answer:**
$$(x + 1)(y - 1) = C \quad \text{or} \quad xy - x + y - 1 = C$$

### Question 5
Find the general solution of the differential equation:
$$y'' - 4y' + 5y = 0$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
For a second-order linear homogeneous ODE with constant coefficients $a y'' + b y' + c y = 0$:
1. **Auxiliary Equation:** $m^2 - 4m + 5 = 0$.
2. **Complex Conjugate Roots:** If roots are $m = \alpha \pm i \beta$, the complementary function is:
   $$y = e^{\alpha x} (c_1 \cos \beta x + c_2 \sin \beta x)$$

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Write and solve the auxiliary equation**
$$m^2 - 4m + 5 = 0$$
Using the quadratic formula $m = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$:

**Step 2: Write the general solution**
Here real part $\alpha = 2$ and imaginary part $\beta = 1$:
$$y = e^{2x} (c_1 \cos x + c_2 \sin x)$$
where $c_1$ and $c_2$ are arbitrary constants.

### Question 6
Define odd and even function with examples.

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
Functions are classified by their algebraic symmetry under reflection across the $y$-axis or inversion through the origin.

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Even Function**
- **Definition:** A function $f(x)$ is even if for all $x$ in its domain:
  $$f(-x) = f(x)$$
- **Geometric Property:** Its graph is symmetric with respect to the $y$-axis.
- **Examples:**
  1. $f(x) = x^2 \implies f(-x) = (-x)^2 = x^2 = f(x)$.
  2. $f(x) = \cos x \implies f(-x) = \cos(-x) = \cos x = f(x)$.
- **Fourier Property:** In a Fourier expansion on $[-\pi, \pi]$, all sine coefficients $b_n = 0$.

**Step 2: Odd Function**
- **Definition:** A function $f(x)$ is odd if for all $x$ in its domain:
  $$f(-x) = -f(x)$$
- **Geometric Property:** Its graph is symmetric with respect to the origin (rotational symmetry of $180^\circ$).
- **Examples:**
  1. $f(x) = x^3 \implies f(-x) = (-x)^3 = -x^3 = -f(x)$.
  2. $f(x) = \sin x \implies f(-x) = \sin(-x) = -\sin x = -f(x)$.
- **Fourier Property:** In a Fourier expansion on $[-\pi, \pi]$, $a_0 = 0$ and all cosine coefficients $a_n = 0$.

### Question 7
If $f(z)$ is differentiable at $z_0$, then show that $f(z)$ is continuous at $z = z_0$.

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Differentiability:** $f'(z_0) = \lim_{z \to z_0} \frac{f(z) - f(z_0)}{z - z_0}$ exists and is finite.
2. **Continuity:** A function $f(z)$ is continuous at $z_0$ if:
   $$\lim_{z \to z_0} f(z) = f(z_0) \iff \lim_{z \to z_0} [f(z) - f(z_0)] = 0$$

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Express the difference $f(z) - f(z_0)$**
For any $z \ne z_0$:
$$f(z) - f(z_0) = \frac{f(z) - f(z_0)}{z - z_0} \cdot (z - z_0)$$

**Step 2: Take the limit as $z \to z_0$**
$$\lim_{z \to z_0} [f(z) - f(z_0)] = \lim_{z \to z_0} \left[ \frac{f(z) - f(z_0)}{z - z_0} \cdot (z - z_0) \right]$$
Using the product rule for limits:
$$= \left( \lim_{z \to z_0} \frac{f(z) - f(z_0)}{z - z_0} \right) \cdot \left( \lim_{z \to z_0} (z - z_0) \right)$$

**Step 3: Evaluate using differentiability**
Since $f(z)$ is differentiable at $z_0$, the first limit is $f'(z_0)$ (a finite number), and the second limit is $0$:
$$\lim_{z \to z_0} [f(z) - f(z_0)] = f'(z_0) \cdot 0 = 0$$

**Step 4: Conclude continuity**
$$\lim_{z \to z_0} f(z) = f(z_0)$$
Hence, $f(z)$ is continuous at $z = z_0$. *(Note: The converse is not necessarily true, e.g., $f(z) = \bar{z}$ is continuous everywhere but nowhere differentiable).*

### Question 9
Define isolated singularity with example.

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
A point $z = z_0$ where a function ceases to be analytic is a singularity.

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Definition of Isolated Singularity**
A point $z = z_0$ is called an **isolated singularity** of a complex function $f(z)$ if:
1. $f(z)$ is not analytic at $z = z_0$.
2. There exists a deleted neighborhood (punctured disk) $0 < |z - z_0| < R$ around $z_0$ in which $f(z)$ is analytic everywhere.
*(In contrast, a non-isolated singularity has other singularities in every neighborhood around it, such as $z = 0$ for $f(z) = \csc(1/z)$).*

**Step 2: Example**
Consider the rational function:
$$f(z) = \frac{1}{z - 2}$$
- $f(z)$ is not analytic at $z = 2$ because the denominator vanishes.
- However, in any punctured disk $0 < |z - 2| < R$, $f(z)$ is differentiable and analytic everywhere.
- Therefore, $z = 2$ is an isolated singularity (specifically, a simple pole).

### Question 10
Show that the function:
$$f(z) = \frac{z^2 - 2z + 5}{z - 2} \quad \text{has a simple pole at } z = 2.$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
A point $z = z_0$ is a **pole of order $m$** of $f(z)$ if:
$$\lim_{z \to z_0} (z - z_0)^m f(z) = L \quad (\text{where } L \ne 0 \text{ is finite})$$
For a simple pole, $m = 1$.

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Set $m = 1$ and take the limit**
Multiply $f(z)$ by $(z - 2)^1$:
$$\lim_{z \to 2} (z - 2) f(z) = \lim_{z \to 2} (z - 2) \cdot \frac{z^2 - 2z + 5}{z - 2}$$
Cancel the common factor $(z - 2)$:
$$= \lim_{z \to 2} (z^2 - 2z + 5)$$

**Step 2: Evaluate the limit**
Substitute $z = 2$:
$$= 2^2 - 2(2) + 5 = 4 - 4 + 5 = 5$$

**Step 3: Conclude pole classification**
Since the limit is non-zero ($5 \ne 0$) and finite, $z = 2$ is a pole of order 1 (a simple pole), and the residue at $z = 2$ is $5$.

### Question 11
Solve:
$$x \, dy - y \, dx = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \, dx$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Homogeneous ODE:** Collect $dx$ and $dy$:
   $$x \, dy = (y + \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}) \, dx \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{y + \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}}{x} = \frac{y}{x} + \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{y}{x}\right)^2}$$
2. **Substitution:** Put $y = vx \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = v + x \frac{dv}{dx}$.
3. **Standard Integral:** $\int \frac{dv}{\sqrt{1 + v^2}} = \ln|v + \sqrt{1 + v^2}| + C$.

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Write in derivative form**
$$x \frac{dy}{dx} - y = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{y}{x} + \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{y}{x}\right)^2}$$

**Step 2: Substitute $y = vx$**
$$\frac{dy}{dx} = v + x \frac{dv}{dx}$$
Substitute into the differential equation:
$$v + x \frac{dv}{dx} = v + \sqrt{1 + v^2}$$
Subtract $v$ from both sides:
$$x \frac{dv}{dx} = \sqrt{1 + v^2}$$

**Step 3: Separate variables**
$$\frac{dv}{\sqrt{1 + v^2}} = \frac{dx}{x}$$

**Step 4: Integrate both sides**
$$\int \frac{dv}{\sqrt{1 + v^2}} = \int \frac{dx}{x}$$
$$\ln|v + \sqrt{1 + v^2}| = \ln|x| + \ln C$$
Combine terms:
$$v + \sqrt{1 + v^2} = C x$$

**Step 5: Substitute back $v = \frac{y}{x}$**
$$\frac{y}{x} + \sqrt{1 + \frac{y^2}{x^2}} = C x$$
Multiply throughout by $x$:
$$y + \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = C x^2$$

**Final Answer:**
$$y + \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = C x^2$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed

### Question 13
Express:
$$f(x) = |x|, \quad -\pi < x < \pi \quad \text{as Fourier series.}$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Even Function Symmetry:** Since $|-x| = |x|$, $f(x)$ is even on $[-\pi, \pi]$.
   - $b_n = 0$ for all $n$.
   - $a_0 = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^\pi f(x) \, dx$.
   - $a_n = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^\pi f(x) \cos(nx) \, dx$.

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Calculate $a_0$**
$$a_0 = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^\pi x \, dx = \frac{2}{\pi} \left[ \frac{x^2}{2} \right]_0^\pi = \frac{2}{\pi} \cdot \frac{\pi^2}{2} = \pi$$

**Step 2: Calculate $a_n$**
$$a_n = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^\pi x \cos(nx) \, dx$$
Integrate by parts ($u = x, dv = \cos(nx) \, dx$):
$$a_n = \frac{2}{\pi} \left[ x \frac{\sin(nx)}{n} - \int \frac{\sin(nx)}{n} \, dx \right]_0^\pi = \frac{2}{\pi} \left[ \frac{x \sin(nx)}{n} + \frac{\cos(nx)}{n^2} \right]_0^\pi$$
Since $\sin(n\pi) = 0$ and $\sin(0) = 0$:
$$a_n = \frac{2}{\pi} \left[ \frac{\cos(n\pi)}{n^2} - \frac{\cos(0)}{n^2} \right] = \frac{2}{\pi n^2} [(-1)^n - 1]$$
- If $n$ is even: $(-1)^n = 1 \implies a_n = 0$.
- If $n$ is odd ($n = 2k - 1$): $(-1)^n = -1 \implies a_n = \frac{2(-2)}{\pi n^2} = -\frac{4}{\pi n^2}$.

**Step 3: Assemble the Fourier Series**
$$f(x) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n \cos(nx)$$
$$f(x) = \frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{4}{\pi} \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{\cos((2k-1)x)}{(2k-1)^2}$$
$$|x| = \frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{4}{\pi} \left( \frac{\cos x}{1^2} + \frac{\cos 3x}{3^2} + \frac{\cos 5x}{5^2} + \dots \right)$$

### Question 14
Find the Fourier transform of:
$$f(x) = \begin{cases} 1 - x^2, & |x| \le 1 \\ 0, & |x| > 1 \end{cases}$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Fourier Transform Definition:**
   $$F(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x) e^{-i\omega x} \, dx = \int_{-1}^1 (1 - x^2) e^{-i\omega x} \, dx$$
2. **Even Function Property:** $e^{-i\omega x} = \cos(\omega x) - i \sin(\omega x)$. Since $(1 - x^2)$ is even, the sine term vanishes:
   $$F(\omega) = 2 \int_0^1 (1 - x^2) \cos(\omega x) \, dx$$

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Set up integration by parts (Tabular / Bernoulli's Rule)**
$$\int u v \, dx = u v_1 - u' v_2 + u'' v_3 - \dots$$
Let $u = 1 - x^2 \implies u' = -2x \implies u'' = -2 \implies u''' = 0$.
Let $v = \cos(\omega x) \implies v_1 = \frac{\sin(\omega x)}{\omega} \implies v_2 = -\frac{\cos(\omega x)}{\omega^2} \implies v_3 = -\frac{\sin(\omega x)}{\omega^3}$.

**Step 2: Evaluate the integral**
$$\int_0^1 (1 - x^2) \cos(\omega x) \, dx = \left[ (1 - x^2)\frac{\sin(\omega x)}{\omega} - (-2x)\left(-\frac{\cos(\omega x)}{\omega^2}\right) + (-2)\left(-\frac{\sin(\omega x)}{\omega^3}\right) \right]_0^1$$
$$= \left[ (1 - x^2)\frac{\sin(\omega x)}{\omega} - \frac{2x \cos(\omega x)}{\omega^2} + \frac{2 \sin(\omega x)}{\omega^3} \right]_0^1$$
Substitute upper limit $x = 1$:
$$= 0 - \frac{2 \cos \omega}{\omega^2} + \frac{2 \sin \omega}{\omega^3}$$
Substitute lower limit $x = 0$:
$$= 0 - 0 + 0 = 0$$
So the inner integral is:
$$\frac{2 \sin \omega - 2\omega \cos \omega}{\omega^3}$$

**Step 3: Multiply by 2**
$$F(\omega) = 2 \left( \frac{2 \sin \omega - 2\omega \cos \omega}{\omega^3} \right) = \frac{4}{\omega^3} (\sin \omega - \omega \cos \omega)$$

### Question 15
Expand:
$$f(z) = \frac{1}{z^2 - 3z + 2} \quad \text{in region: (a) } |z| < 1, \quad \text{(b) } 1 < |z| < 2$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Partial Fractions:** $\frac{1}{(z - 1)(z - 2)} = \frac{1}{z - 2} - \frac{1}{z - 1}$.
2. **Geometric Series:** $\frac{1}{1 - w} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty w^n$ for $|w| < 1$.

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Partial fraction decomposition**
$$\frac{1}{z^2 - 3z + 2} = \frac{1}{(z - 1)(z - 2)} = \frac{A}{z - 1} + \frac{B}{z - 2}$$
$1 = A(z - 2) + B(z - 1)$.
- Put $z = 1 \implies A = -1$.
- Put $z = 2 \implies B = 1$.
$$f(z) = \frac{1}{z - 2} - \frac{1}{z - 1} = \frac{1}{1 - z} - \frac{1}{2\left(1 - \frac{z}{2}\right)}$$

**Step 2: Region (a): $|z| < 1$**
Both $|z| < 1$ and $|z/2| < 1/2 < 1$:
$$\frac{1}{1 - z} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty z^n$$
$$\frac{1}{2\left(1 - \frac{z}{2}\right)} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{n=0}^\infty \left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^n = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{z^n}{2^{n+1}}$$
Subtracting gives:
$$f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \left( 1 - \frac{1}{2^{n+1}} \right) z^n$$

**Step 3: Region (b): $1 < |z| < 2$**
- Since $|z| > 1 \implies |1/z| < 1$:
  $$\frac{1}{1 - z} = -\frac{1}{z\left(1 - \frac{1}{z}\right)} = -\frac{1}{z} \sum_{n=0}^\infty \left(\frac{1}{z}\right)^n = -\sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{1}{z^{n+1}} = -\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{z^n}$$
- Since $|z| < 2 \implies |z/2| < 1$:
  $$-\frac{1}{2\left(1 - \frac{z}{2}\right)} = -\sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{z^n}{2^{n+1}}$$
Combining both parts:
$$f(z) = -\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{z^n} - \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{z^n}{2^{n+1}}$$

### Question 17
Solve:
$$\frac{dy}{dx} + y \tan x = \sec x$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
First-order linear differential equation: $\frac{dy}{dx} + P(x) y = Q(x)$.
1. Integrating factor: $I.F. = e^{\int P(x) \, dx}$.
2. General solution: $y \cdot (I.F.) = \int Q(x) \cdot (I.F.) \, dx + C$.

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Identify $P(x)$ and $Q(x)$**
$$P(x) = \tan x, \quad Q(x) = \sec x$$

**Step 2: Find the Integrating Factor**
$$I.F. = e^{\int \tan x \, dx} = e^{\ln|\sec x|} = \sec x$$

**Step 3: Multiply and integrate**
$$y \cdot \sec x = \int \sec x \cdot \sec x \, dx = \int \sec^2 x \, dx$$
$$y \sec x = \tan x + C$$

**Step 4: Solve for $y$**
Divide throughout by $\sec x$ (or multiply by $\cos x$):
$$y = \frac{\tan x}{\sec x} + \frac{C}{\sec x} = \sin x + C \cos x$$

### Question 18
Evaluate:
$$\int_0^\infty \int_0^x \frac{e^{-x}}{x} \, dy \, dx \quad \text{by changing the order of integration.}$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. The given limits are:
   - Inner: $y = 0$ to $y = x$.
   - Outer: $x = 0$ to $x = \infty$.
2. Region of integration: The wedge between the positive $x$-axis ($y = 0$) and the line $y = x$ as $x \to \infty$.
3. When reversing order: For a fixed $y \ge 0$, $x$ varies from $x = y$ to $x = \infty$. The range of $y$ is $0 \le y < \infty$.

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Change the order of integration**
$$\int_{y=0}^\infty \left[ \int_{x=y}^\infty \frac{e^{-x}}{x} \, dx \right] dy$$

**Step 2: Reverse iterated evaluation**
Notice that integrating the inner integral directly gives:
$$\int_0^\infty \left[ \int_0^x \frac{e^{-x}}{x} \, dy \right] dx = \int_0^\infty \frac{e^{-x}}{x} [y]_0^x \, dx = \int_0^\infty \frac{e^{-x}}{x} (x) \, dx$$
$$= \int_0^\infty e^{-x} \, dx = \left[ -e^{-x} \right]_0^\infty = 0 - (-1) = 1$$
Under reversed order:
$$\int_0^\infty dy \int_y^\infty \frac{e^{-x}}{x} \, dx = \int_0^\infty dx \int_0^x \frac{e^{-x}}{x} \, dy = \int_0^\infty e^{-x} \, dx = 1$$

**Final Answer:**
$$\int_0^\infty \int_0^x \frac{e^{-x}}{x} \, dy \, dx = 1$$

### Question 19
Show that:
$$u = e^x (x \cos y - y \sin y) \quad \text{is a harmonic function and find the analytic function } f(z) = u + iv.$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Harmonic condition:** $\nabla^2 u = \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2} = 0$.
2. **Milne-Thomson Method:** $f'(z) = u_x(z, 0) - i u_y(z, 0)$. Integrate to get $f(z)$.

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: First and second derivatives with respect to $x$**
$$u = e^x(x \cos y - y \sin y)$$
$$u_x = e^x(x \cos y - y \sin y) + e^x(\cos y) = e^x(x \cos y - y \sin y + \cos y)$$
$$u_{xx} = e^x(x \cos y - y \sin y + \cos y) + e^x(\cos y) = e^x(x \cos y - y \sin y + 2\cos y)$$

**Step 2: First and second derivatives with respect to $y$**
$$u_y = e^x(-x \sin y - \sin y - y \cos y)$$
$$u_{yy} = e^x(-x \cos y - \cos y - \cos y + y \sin y) = e^x(-x \cos y + y \sin y - 2\cos y)$$

**Step 3: Verify Laplace's Equation**
$$u_{xx} + u_{yy} = e^x(x \cos y - y \sin y + 2\cos y) + e^x(-x \cos y + y \sin y - 2\cos y) = 0$$
Hence, $u$ is harmonic everywhere.

**Step 4: Find $f(z)$ using Milne-Thomson Method**
Evaluate $u_x$ and $u_y$ at $(x = z, y = 0)$:
$$u_x(z, 0) = e^z(z(1) - 0 + 1) = (z + 1)e^z$$
$$u_y(z, 0) = e^z(0 - 0 - 0) = 0$$
Therefore:
$$f'(z) = u_x(z, 0) - i u_y(z, 0) = (z + 1)e^z$$

**Step 5: Integrate $f'(z)$**
$$f(z) = \int (z + 1)e^z \, dz = z e^z + C$$

### Question 20
The equations of motion of a particle are given by:
$$\frac{dx}{dt} + \omega y = 0, \quad \frac{dy}{dt} - \omega x = 0$$
Find the path of the particle and show that it is a circle.

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. Coupled system of first-order linear ODEs:
   - (1) $\frac{dx}{dt} = -\omega y$
   - (2) $\frac{dy}{dt} = \omega x$
2. Differentiate one equation to eliminate the other variable, producing a simple harmonic motion ODE.

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Differentiate equation (1) with respect to $t$**
$$\frac{d^2 x}{dt^2} + \omega \frac{dy}{dt} = 0$$

**Step 2: Substitute $\frac{dy}{dt} = \omega x$ from equation (2)**
$$\frac{d^2 x}{dt^2} + \omega (\omega x) = 0 \implies \frac{d^2 x}{dt^2} + \omega^2 x = 0$$

**Step 3: Solve for $x(t)$**
The auxiliary equation is $m^2 + \omega^2 = 0 \implies m = \pm i\omega$:
$$x(t) = R \cos(\omega t + \alpha)$$
where $R$ and $\alpha$ are constants of integration.

**Step 4: Find $y(t)$ using equation (1)**
From (1): $y = -\frac{1}{\omega} \frac{dx}{dt}$.
$$\frac{dx}{dt} = -R\omega \sin(\omega t + \alpha)$$
$$y(t) = -\frac{1}{\omega} (-R\omega \sin(\omega t + \alpha)) = R \sin(\omega t + \alpha)$$

**Step 5: Eliminate the parameter $t$**
Square and add $x(t)$ and $y(t)$:
$$x^2 + y^2 = R^2 \cos^2(\omega t + \alpha) + R^2 \sin^2(\omega t + \alpha) = R^2 (\cos^2(\omega t + \alpha) + \sin^2(\omega t + \alpha))$$
$$x^2 + y^2 = R^2$$
This represents the equation of a circle with center at the origin $(0, 0)$ and radius $R$. Hence, the path of the particle is a circle.

### Question 22
Find the residue of:
$$f(z) = \frac{3z - 4}{z(z - 1)(z - 2)} \quad \text{at each of its poles.}$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. The denominator vanishes at $z = 0, 1, 2$. Each is a simple pole (order 1).
2. Residue formula for a simple pole at $z = z_0$:
   $$\text{Res}(f, z_0) = \lim_{z \to z_0} (z - z_0) f(z)$$

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Residue at pole $z = 0$**
$$\text{Res}(f, 0) = \lim_{z \to 0} z \cdot \frac{3z - 4}{z(z - 1)(z - 2)} = \frac{3(0) - 4}{(0 - 1)(0 - 2)} = \frac{-4}{(-1)(-2)} = \frac{-4}{2} = -2$$

**Step 2: Residue at pole $z = 1$**
$$\text{Res}(f, 1) = \lim_{z \to 1} (z - 1) \cdot \frac{3z - 4}{z(z - 1)(z - 2)} = \frac{3(1) - 4}{1(1 - 2)} = \frac{-1}{1(-1)} = 1$$

**Step 3: Residue at pole $z = 2$**
$$\text{Res}(f, 2) = \lim_{z \to 2} (z - 2) \cdot \frac{3z - 4}{z(z - 1)(z - 2)} = \frac{3(2) - 4}{2(2 - 1)} = \frac{6 - 4}{2(1)} = \frac{2}{2} = 1$$

### Question 23
Find the Fourier transform of:
$$f(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & |x| < 1 \\ 0, & |x| > 1 \end{cases}$$
Hence, evaluate:
$$\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin s}{s} \, ds$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Fourier Transform:** $F(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x) e^{-i\omega x} \, dx$.
2. **Inverse Fourier Transform (Inversion Theorem):**
   $$f(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^\infty F(\omega) e^{i\omega x} \, d\omega$$

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Compute $F(\omega)$**
$$F(\omega) = \int_{-1}^1 1 \cdot e^{-i\omega x} \, dx = \left[ \frac{e^{-i\omega x}}{-i\omega} \right]_{-1}^1 = \frac{e^{-i\omega} - e^{i\omega}}{-i\omega} = \frac{e^{i\omega} - e^{-i\omega}}{i\omega}$$
Using $\sin \omega = \frac{e^{i\omega} - e^{-i\omega}}{2i}$:
$$F(\omega) = \frac{2i \sin \omega}{i\omega} = \frac{2\sin \omega}{\omega}$$

**Step 2: Apply the Inversion Formula**
$$f(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{2\sin \omega}{\omega} e^{i\omega x} \, d\omega = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{\sin \omega}{\omega} (\cos(\omega x) + i \sin(\omega x)) \, d\omega$$
Since $\frac{\sin \omega}{\omega}$ is an even function, the sine term vanishes:
$$f(x) = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^\infty \frac{\sin \omega \cos(\omega x)}{\omega} \, d\omega$$

**Step 3: Evaluate at $x = 0$**
At $x = 0$, $f(x) = 1$ (which lies in $|x| < 1$, where $f$ is continuous):
$$1 = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^\infty \frac{\sin \omega}{\omega} \, d\omega$$
Multiplying by $\frac{\pi}{2}$:
$$\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin \omega}{\omega} \, d\omega = \frac{\pi}{2}$$
Changing dummy variable to $s$:
$$\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin s}{s} \, ds = \frac{\pi}{2}$$

### Question 24
Find the Taylor expansion of:
$$\frac{2z^3 + 1}{z^2 + 2} \quad \text{about the point } z = i.$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Taylor Series Formula:**
   $$f(z) = f(i) + f'(i)(z - i) + \frac{f''(i)}{2!}(z - i)^2 + \dots$$
2. Alternatively, substitute $w = z - i \implies z = w + i$ and expand about $w = 0$.

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Evaluate $f(i)$**
Substitute $z = i$:
$$f(i) = \frac{2(i)^3 + 1}{i^2 + 2} = \frac{2(-i) + 1}{-1 + 2} = \frac{1 - 2i}{1} = 1 - 2i$$

**Step 2: Differentiate $f(z)$ using the quotient rule**
$$f(z) = \frac{u}{v}, \quad u = 2z^3 + 1, \quad v = z^2 + 2$$
$$u' = 6z^2, \quad v' = 2z$$
$$f'(z) = \frac{u' v - u v'}{v^2} = \frac{6z^2(z^2 + 2) - (2z^3 + 1)(2z)}{(z^2 + 2)^2} = \frac{6z^4 + 12z^2 - (4z^4 + 2z)}{(z^2 + 2)^2} = \frac{2z^4 + 12z^2 - 2z}{(z^2 + 2)^2}$$

**Step 3: Evaluate $f'(i)$**
Substitute $z = i$ (note $i^2 = -1, i^4 = 1$):
$$f'(i) = \frac{2(1) + 12(-1) - 2i}{(-1 + 2)^2} = \frac{2 - 12 - 2i}{1^2} = -10 - 2i$$

**Step 4: Write the Taylor series up to linear term**
$$f(z) = f(i) + f'(i)(z - i) + \mathcal{O}((z - i)^2)$$
$$f(z) = (1 - 2i) - (10 + 2i)(z - i) + \dots$$

**Step 5: Radius of convergence**
Poles of $f(z)$ occur where $z^2 + 2 = 0 \implies z = \pm i\sqrt{2}$.
Distance from expansion center $z = i$ to nearest pole $z = i\sqrt{2}$:
$$R = |i\sqrt{2} - i| = (\sqrt{2} - 1) \approx 0.414$$
The Taylor series converges in the disk $|z - i| < \sqrt{2} - 1$.

### Question 25
Define Fourier cosine series and Fourier sine series with Dirichlet conditions.

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
Half-range expansions represent an arbitrary function $f(x)$ defined only on the half-interval $[0, L]$.

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Fourier Cosine Series**
- If $f(x)$ is extended to $[-L, L]$ as an **even function** ($f(-x) = f(x)$), all sine coefficients $b_n = 0$.
- The half-range cosine series on $[0, L]$ is:
  $$f(x) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n \cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$$
  where:
  $$a_0 = \frac{2}{L} \int_0^L f(x) \, dx, \quad a_n = \frac{2}{L} \int_0^L f(x) \cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) dx$$

**Step 2: Fourier Sine Series**
- If $f(x)$ is extended to $[-L, L]$ as an **odd function** ($f(-x) = -f(x)$), all cosine coefficients $a_0 = a_n = 0$.
- The half-range sine series on $[0, L]$ is:
  $$f(x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$$
  where:
  $$b_n = \frac{2}{L} \int_0^L f(x) \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) dx$$

**Step 3: Dirichlet Conditions for Convergence**
A function $f(x)$ can be expanded in a convergent Fourier series in any interval of length $2L$ if it satisfies the three **Dirichlet Conditions**:
1. **Single-valued & Bounded:** $f(x)$ is single-valued and bounded everywhere in the interval.
2. **Piecewise Continuous:** $f(x)$ has at most a finite number of finite (jump) discontinuities in any one period.
3. **Finite Extrema:** $f(x)$ has at most a finite number of local maxima and minima in any one period.
Under these conditions:
- At any point of continuity $x_0$, the Fourier series converges to $f(x_0)$.
- At any jump discontinuity $x_d$, the series converges to the average of the left and right limits:
  $$\frac{f(x_d^+) + f(x_d^-)}{2}$$
