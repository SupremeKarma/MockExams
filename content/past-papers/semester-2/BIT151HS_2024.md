### Question 1
Evaluate:
$$\int_0^a \int_0^a \int_0^a (x + y + z) \, dx \, dy \, dz$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Triple Iterated Integral:** A triple integral over a rectangular box $[0, a] \times [0, a] \times [0, a]$ is evaluated from the inside out:
   $$\int_{z=0}^a \left( \int_{y=0}^a \left( \int_{x=0}^a f(x, y, z) \, dx \right) dy \right) dz$$
2. **Partial Integration:** When integrating with respect to $x$, treat other variables ($y$ and $z$) as constants.
3. **Power Rule of Integration:**
   $$\int x^n \, dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C \quad (n \ne -1), \qquad \int k \, dx = kx + C$$
4. **Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:**
   $$\int_A^B f(x) \, dx = [F(x)]_A^B = F(B) - F(A)$$

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Write down the integral with explicit variable limits**
$$I = \int_{z=0}^a \int_{y=0}^a \int_{x=0}^a (x + y + z) \, dx \, dy \, dz$$

**Step 2: Evaluate the innermost integral with respect to $x$**
Treat $y$ and $z$ as constants during this integration:
$$\int_{x=0}^a (x + y + z) \, dx = \left[ \frac{x^2}{2} + yx + zx \right]_{x=0}^{x=a}$$

Substitute the upper limit $x = a$ and lower limit $x = 0$:
$$= \left( \frac{a^2}{2} + ya + za \right) - \left( \frac{0^2}{2} + y(0) + z(0) \right)$$
$$= \frac{a^2}{2} + a y + a z$$

**Step 3: Evaluate the middle integral with respect to $y$**
Substitute the result from Step 2 into the $y$-integral. Here, $a$ and $z$ are treated as constants:
$$\int_{y=0}^a \left( \frac{a^2}{2} + ay + az \right) \, dy = \left[ \frac{a^2}{2} y + a \frac{y^2}{2} + az y \right]_{y=0}^{y=a}$$

Substitute upper limit $y = a$ and lower limit $y = 0$:
$$= \left( \frac{a^2}{2}(a) + a \frac{a^2}{2} + az(a) \right) - (0 + 0 + 0)$$
$$= \frac{a^3}{2} + \frac{a^3}{2} + a^2 z$$
$$= a^3 + a^2 z$$

**Step 4: Evaluate the outermost integral with respect to $z$**
Substitute the result from Step 3 into the $z$-integral:
$$I = \int_{z=0}^a (a^3 + a^2 z) \, dz = \left[ a^3 z + a^2 \frac{z^2}{2} \right]_{z=0}^{z=a}$$

Substitute upper limit $z = a$ and lower limit $z = 0$:
$$I = \left( a^3(a) + a^2 \frac{a^2}{2} \right) - (0 + 0)$$
$$I = a^4 + \frac{a^4}{2} = \frac{2a^4 + a^4}{2} = \frac{3a^4}{2}$$

---

#### ✅ Final Answer
$$\int_0^a \int_0^a \int_0^a (x + y + z) \, dx \, dy \, dz = \frac{3a^4}{2}$$

> ⚠️ **Beginner Tip:** Don't forget that when integrating a constant term like $y$ with respect to $x$, the result is $yx$, not $\frac{y^2}{2}$. Only integrate the active variable!

### Question 2
Evaluate the integral by changing the order of integration:
$$\int_0^\infty \int_x^\infty \frac{e^{-y}}{y} \, dy \, dx$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Why change the order?** The inner integral $\int_x^\infty \frac{e^{-y}}{y} \, dy$ cannot be evaluated in elementary functions (it is an Exponential Integral $E_1(x)$). By swapping the order to integrate with respect to $x$ first, the factor $\frac{1}{y}$ cancels out!
2. **Order Reversal Technique:**
   - Write out the given boundary inequalities for the integration region $R$.
   - Sketch or describe the region in the $xy$-plane.
   - Re-express the region with horizontal strips ($x$ running from left boundary to right boundary, then $y$ from bottom to top).

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Identify the region of integration $R$ from given limits**
From the integral $\int_{x=0}^\infty \int_{y=x}^\infty \frac{e^{-y}}{y} \, dy \, dx$:
- Inner limits: $y$ goes from $y = x$ to $y = \infty$.
- Outer limits: $x$ goes from $x = 0$ to $x = \infty$.

**Step 2: Understand the geometry of region $R$**
- The lower boundary is the straight line $y = x$.
- The left boundary is the $y$-axis ($x = 0$).
- The region extends infinitely upwards ($y \to \infty$) and to the right ($x \to \infty$) such that $y \ge x \ge 0$.

**Step 3: Change the order to integrate with respect to $x$ first**
Look at horizontal slices at a fixed height $y$:
- $x$ starts at the left boundary $x = 0$ (the $y$-axis).
- $x$ ends at the line $x = y$.
- $y$ ranges over the entire vertical span: from $y = 0$ to $y = \infty$.

Therefore, the new limits are:
$$0 \le x \le y, \qquad 0 \le y < \infty$$

**Step 4: Rewrite and evaluate the transformed integral**
$$I = \int_{y=0}^\infty \left( \int_{x=0}^y \frac{e^{-y}}{y} \, dx \right) dy$$

Evaluate the inner integral with respect to $x$ (treating $y$ as a constant):
$$\int_{x=0}^y \frac{e^{-y}}{y} \, dx = \frac{e^{-y}}{y} \int_{x=0}^y 1 \, dx = \frac{e^{-y}}{y} [x]_0^y = \frac{e^{-y}}{y} (y - 0) = e^{-y}$$

Notice how nicely the $y$ in the denominator cancelled out!

**Step 5: Evaluate the outer integral with respect to $y$**
$$I = \int_0^\infty e^{-y} \, dy = \left[ -e^{-y} \right]_0^\infty$$
$$I = \left( -e^{-\infty} \right) - \left( -e^{-0} \right) = (-0) - (-1) = 1$$

---

#### ✅ Final Answer
$$\int_0^\infty \int_x^\infty \frac{e^{-y}}{y} \, dy \, dx = 1$$

> ⚠️ **Beginner Tip:** Always remember $e^{-\infty} = \lim_{y \to \infty} \frac{1}{e^y} = 0$, and $e^0 = 1$.

### Question 3
Determine the order and degree of the differential equation:
$$\sqrt{\frac{d^3 y}{dx^3}} = \frac{dy}{dx}$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Order of a Differential Equation:** The order of the highest derivative occurring in the equation.
2. **Degree of a Differential Equation:** The highest exponent (power) of the highest order derivative after the equation is made free from radicals (fractional powers like square roots, cube roots) and fractions involving derivatives.

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Check for radicals on the derivatives**
The given equation contains a square root on the derivative:
$$\sqrt{\frac{d^3 y}{dx^3}} = \frac{dy}{dx}$$
This means the derivative is raised to the power $\frac{1}{2}$:
$$\left(\frac{d^3 y}{dx^3}\right)^{1/2} = \frac{dy}{dx}$$

**Step 2: Clear the radical by squaring both sides**
$$\left(\sqrt{\frac{d^3 y}{dx^3}}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2$$
$$\frac{d^3 y}{dx^3} = \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2$$

**Step 3: Identify the Order**
- The derivatives present are $\frac{d^3 y}{dx^3}$ (3rd derivative) and $\frac{dy}{dx}$ (1st derivative).
- The highest derivative is $\frac{d^3 y}{dx^3}$.
- Hence, **$\text{Order} = 3$**.

**Step 4: Identify the Degree**
- In the rationalized polynomial form $\left(\frac{d^3 y}{dx^3}\right)^1 = \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2$, the power of the highest order derivative $\frac{d^3 y}{dx^3}$ is $1$.
- Hence, **$\text{Degree} = 1$**.

---

#### ✅ Final Answer
$$\text{Order} = 3, \qquad \text{Degree} = 1$$

> ⚠️ **Beginner Tip:** Do not say the degree is 2! The power 2 belongs to the first derivative $\frac{dy}{dx}$, not the highest derivative $\frac{d^3 y}{dx^3}$. Degree is strictly the power of the highest order derivative.

### Question 4
Solve:
$$(x + 1) \, dy = (y - 1) \, dx$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Variable Separable Method:** When a differential equation can be written such that all terms containing $y$ are with $dy$ and all terms containing $x$ are with $dx$:
   $$g(y) \, dy = f(x) \, dx \implies \int g(y) \, dy = \int f(x) \, dx + C$$
2. **Logarithmic Integration Rule:**
   $$\int \frac{1}{u} \, du = \ln|u| + C$$
3. **Logarithm Rules:** $\ln A + \ln B = \ln(AB)$, and $e^{\ln u} = u$.

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Separate the variables**
Given:
$$(x + 1) \, dy = (y - 1) \, dx$$

Divide both sides by $(x + 1)(y - 1)$ (assuming $x \ne -1, y \ne 1$):
$$\frac{dy}{y - 1} = \frac{dx}{x + 1}$$

**Step 2: Integrate both sides**
$$\int \frac{1}{y - 1} \, dy = \int \frac{1}{x + 1} \, dx$$

Evaluating both standard integrals:
$$\ln|y - 1| = \ln|x + 1| + C_1$$

**Step 3: Simplify using properties of logarithms**
To combine the constant nicely, write the arbitrary constant $C_1$ as $\ln|C|$ (where $C > 0$):
$$\ln|y - 1| = \ln|x + 1| + \ln|C|$$
$$\ln|y - 1| = \ln|C(x + 1)|$$

Taking the exponential of both sides ($e^{\ln A} = A$):
$$|y - 1| = |C(x + 1)| \implies y - 1 = C(x + 1)$$
$$y = C(x + 1) + 1$$

---

#### ✅ Final Answer
$$y = C(x + 1) + 1 \quad \text{or} \quad \frac{y - 1}{x + 1} = C$$

### Question 5
Solve:
$$\frac{d^2 y}{dx^2} - 4 \frac{dy}{dx} + 13 y = 0$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Homogeneous Linear Second-Order ODE with Constant Coefficients:**
   $$a \frac{d^2 y}{dx^2} + b \frac{dy}{dx} + c y = 0$$
2. **Auxiliary Equation (A.E.):** Substitute $y = e^{mx} \implies a m^2 + b m + c = 0$.
3. **Complex Conjugate Roots Rule:** If the roots are $m = \alpha \pm i\beta$, the general solution is:
   $$y = e^{\alpha x} (c_1 \cos\beta x + c_2 \sin\beta x)$$

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Write down the auxiliary equation**
Replace $\frac{d^2 y}{dx^2}$ with $m^2$, $\frac{dy}{dx}$ with $m$, and $y$ with $1$:
$$m^2 - 4m + 13 = 0$$

**Step 2: Solve for $m$ using the quadratic formula**
For $a = 1, b = -4, c = 13$:
$$m = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a} = \frac{-(-4) \pm \sqrt{(-4)^2 - 4(1)(13)}}{2(1)}$$
$$m = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{16 - 52}}{2} = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{-36}}{2}$$

Since $\sqrt{-36} = \sqrt{36} \cdot \sqrt{-1} = 6i$:
$$m = \frac{4 \pm 6i}{2} = 2 \pm 3i$$

**Step 3: Extract real part ($\alpha$) and imaginary part ($\beta$)**
$$\alpha = 2, \qquad \beta = 3$$

**Step 4: Form the general solution**
Using the complex roots formula:
$$y = e^{\alpha x} (c_1 \cos\beta x + c_2 \sin\beta x)$$
$$y = e^{2x} (c_1 \cos 3x + c_2 \sin 3x)$$

where $c_1$ and $c_2$ are arbitrary constants.

---

#### ✅ Final Answer
$$y = e^{2x} (c_1 \cos 3x + c_2 \sin 3x)$$

### Question 6
Determine whether the function $f(x) = \log\left|\frac{1 - x}{1 + x}\right|$ is even or odd.

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Definition of Even Function:** $f(-x) = f(x)$ for all $x$ in the domain (symmetric about $y$-axis).
2. **Definition of Odd Function:** $f(-x) = -f(x)$ for all $x$ in the domain (symmetric about the origin).
3. **Logarithm Reciprocal Rule:** $\log\left(\frac{A}{B}\right) = -\log\left(\frac{B}{A}\right)$ because $\frac{A}{B} = \left(\frac{B}{A}\right)^{-1}$.

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Given function**
$$f(x) = \log\left|\frac{1 - x}{1 + x}\right|$$

**Step 2: Replace $x$ with $-x$**
$$f(-x) = \log\left|\frac{1 - (-x)}{1 + (-x)}\right|$$
Since $1 - (-x) = 1 + x$ and $1 + (-x) = 1 - x$:
$$f(-x) = \log\left|\frac{1 + x}{1 - x}\right|$$

**Step 3: Relate $f(-x)$ to $f(x)$**
Notice that the fraction inside the log is the reciprocal of the original fraction:
$$\frac{1 + x}{1 - x} = \left( \frac{1 - x}{1 + x} \right)^{-1}$$

Using the power law of logarithms $\log(u^k) = k \log u$:
$$f(-x) = \log\left| \left( \frac{1 - x}{1 + x} \right)^{-1} \right| = -1 \cdot \log\left|\frac{1 - x}{1 + x}\right|$$
$$f(-x) = -f(x)$$

**Step 4: Conclusion**
Since $f(-x) = -f(x)$, by definition $f(x)$ is an **odd function**.

---

#### ✅ Final Answer
The function $f(x) = \log\left|\frac{1 - x}{1 + x}\right|$ is an **odd function**.

### Question 7
Express the function $f(z) = \sin z$ in the form of $u(x, y) + i v(x, y)$.

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Complex Variable Representation:** $z = x + iy$.
2. **Compound Angle Formula for Sine:**
   $$\sin(A + B) = \sin A \cos B + \cos A \sin B$$
3. **Hyperbolic Relations for Pure Imaginary Arguments:**
   $$\cos(iy) = \cosh y, \qquad \sin(iy) = i \sinh y$$
   *(Recall: $\cosh y = \frac{e^y + e^{-y}}{2}$, $\sinh y = \frac{e^y - e^{-y}}{2}$)*.

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Substitute $z = x + iy$ into $f(z)$**
$$f(z) = \sin(x + iy)$$

**Step 2: Expand using the sine addition formula**
Let $A = x$ and $B = iy$:
$$\sin(x + iy) = \sin x \cos(iy) + \cos x \sin(iy)$$

**Step 3: Convert imaginary arguments to real hyperbolic functions**
Using $\cos(iy) = \cosh y$ and $\sin(iy) = i \sinh y$:
$$\sin(x + iy) = \sin x (\cosh y) + \cos x (i \sinh y)$$
$$\sin(x + iy) = (\sin x \cosh y) + i (\cos x \sinh y)$$

**Step 4: Identify real and imaginary components**
Matching with $f(z) = u(x, y) + i v(x, y)$:
- **Real Part:** $u(x, y) = \sin x \cosh y$
- **Imaginary Part:** $v(x, y) = \cos x \sinh y$

---

#### ✅ Final Answer
$$f(z) = (\sin x \cosh y) + i (\cos x \sinh y)$$
where $u(x, y) = \sin x \cosh y$ and $v(x, y) = \cos x \sinh y$.

### Question 8
If $f(z)$ is differentiable at $z_0$, then show that $f(z)$ is continuous at $z = z_0$.

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Differentiability Definition:** A function $f(z)$ is differentiable at $z_0$ if the limit:
   $$f'(z_0) = \lim_{z \to z_0} \frac{f(z) - f(z_0)}{z - z_0}$$
   exists and is a finite complex number.
2. **Continuity Definition:** A function $f(z)$ is continuous at $z_0$ if:
   $$\lim_{z \to z_0} f(z) = f(z_0) \iff \lim_{z \to z_0} [f(z) - f(z_0)] = 0$$

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: State the hypothesis**
We are given that $f(z)$ is differentiable at $z_0$. Therefore:
$$\lim_{z \to z_0} \frac{f(z) - f(z_0)}{z - z_0} = f'(z_0) \quad \text{(a finite value)}$$

**Step 2: Set up the algebraic identity for $z \ne z_0$**
For any point $z$ in the neighborhood of $z_0$ with $z \ne z_0$, we can multiply and divide by $(z - z_0)$:
$$f(z) - f(z_0) = \left( \frac{f(z) - f(z_0)}{z - z_0} \right) \cdot (z - z_0)$$

**Step 3: Take the limit as $z \to z_0$ on both sides**
$$\lim_{z \to z_0} [f(z) - f(z_0)] = \lim_{z \to z_0} \left[ \left( \frac{f(z) - f(z_0)}{z - z_0} \right) \cdot (z - z_0) \right]$$

**Step 4: Apply the product rule for limits**
$$\lim_{z \to z_0} [f(z) - f(z_0)] = \left( \lim_{z \to z_0} \frac{f(z) - f(z_0)}{z - z_0} \right) \cdot \left( \lim_{z \to z_0} (z - z_0) \right)$$

Substitute the known limit values:
$$\lim_{z \to z_0} [f(z) - f(z_0)] = f'(z_0) \cdot (z_0 - z_0) = f'(z_0) \cdot 0 = 0$$

**Step 5: Conclude continuity**
$$\lim_{z \to z_0} [f(z) - f(z_0)] = 0 \implies \lim_{z \to z_0} f(z) = f(z_0)$$
This is precisely the definition of continuity at $z = z_0$. Hence proved.

### Question 9
Find the zeros of the function:
$$f(z) = \left(\frac{z + 2}{z^2 + 1}\right)^3$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Zeros of a Rational Function:** The zeros of $f(z) = \frac{P(z)}{Q(z)}$ are the roots of the numerator $P(z) = 0$ at which the denominator $Q(z) \ne 0$.
2. **Order of a Zero:** If $f(z) = (z - z_0)^m \phi(z)$ where $\phi(z_0) \ne 0$, then $z = z_0$ is a zero of order (multiplicity) $m$.

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Rewrite $f(z)$ as a quotient**
$$f(z) = \frac{(z + 2)^3}{(z^2 + 1)^3}$$

**Step 2: Find values of $z$ that make the numerator zero**
$$(z + 2)^3 = 0 \implies z + 2 = 0 \implies z = -2$$

**Step 3: Check that the denominator is non-zero at $z = -2$**
Denominator:
$$Q(-2) = ((-2)^2 + 1)^3 = (4 + 1)^3 = 5^3 = 125 \ne 0$$
Since the denominator does not vanish at $z = -2$, $z = -2$ is definitely a zero (and not an indeterminate form).

**Step 4: Determine the order of the zero**
Because the factor $(z + 2)$ is raised to the power $3$:
$$f(z) = (z - (-2))^3 \cdot \frac{1}{(z^2 + 1)^3}$$
where $\phi(z) = \frac{1}{(z^2 + 1)^3}$ satisfies $\phi(-2) = \frac{1}{125} \ne 0$.

Therefore, $z = -2$ is a zero of order 3.

---

#### ✅ Final Answer
The zero of $f(z)$ is $z = -2$ with **multiplicity (order) 3**.

### Question 10
Show that the function:
$$f(z) = \frac{z^2 - 2z + 5}{z - 2} \quad \text{has a simple pole at } z = 2.$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Definition of a Pole of Order $m$:** A function $f(z)$ has a pole of order $m$ at $z = z_0$ if:
   $$\lim_{z \to z_0} (z - z_0)^m f(z) = L \quad \text{where } L \text{ is finite and } L \ne 0$$
2. **Simple Pole:** A pole of order $m = 1$. The value $L$ is precisely the residue $\text{Res}(f, z_0)$.

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Inspect the denominator and numerator at $z = 2$**
Given:
$$f(z) = \frac{z^2 - 2z + 5}{z - 2}$$
- Numerator at $z = 2$: $P(2) = (2)^2 - 2(2) + 5 = 4 - 4 + 5 = 5 \ne 0$.
- Denominator at $z = 2$: $Q(2) = 2 - 2 = 0$.
Since the denominator has a single factor $(z - 2)$ and the numerator does not vanish at $z = 2$, we suspect a simple pole ($m = 1$).

**Step 2: Compute the limit for $m = 1$**
Multiply $f(z)$ by $(z - 2)^1$:
$$\lim_{z \to 2} (z - 2) f(z) = \lim_{z \to 2} \left[ (z - 2) \cdot \frac{z^2 - 2z + 5}{z - 2} \right]$$

Cancel the common factor $(z - 2)$:
$$= \lim_{z \to 2} (z^2 - 2z + 5)$$

Substitute $z = 2$:
$$= (2)^2 - 2(2) + 5 = 4 - 4 + 5 = 5$$

**Step 3: Analyze the result**
The limit is $5$, which is **finite and non-zero** ($5 \ne 0$).
By definition, $z = 2$ is a pole of order $1$, known as a **simple pole**.
Furthermore, the residue at this pole is $\text{Res}(f, 2) = 5$.

### Question 11
Evaluate:
$$\iint_R xy \, dx \, dy \quad \text{where } R \text{ is the positive quadrant of the circle } x^2 + y^2 = a^2.$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Region in Positive Quadrant (First Quadrant):**
   - $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$.
   - Circle boundary: $x^2 + y^2 = a^2 \implies y = \sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$.
2. **Two standard approaches:**
   - **Approach 1 (Cartesian Coordinates):** Vertical strips where $y$ goes from $0$ to $\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$ and $x$ goes from $0$ to $a$.
   - **Approach 2 (Polar Coordinates):** $x = r\cos\theta, y = r\sin\theta, dx dy = r \, dr \, d\theta$.

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

#### Approach 1: Cartesian Coordinates

**Step 1: Set up the limits of integration**
- For any fixed $x$ between $0$ and $a$, $y$ goes from the $x$-axis ($y = 0$) up to the circular arc ($y = \sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$).
- Therefore:
  $$I = \int_{x=0}^a \int_{y=0}^{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}} xy \, dy \, dx$$

**Step 2: Evaluate the inner integral with respect to $y$**
Treat $x$ as constant:
$$\int_{y=0}^{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}} xy \, dy = x \left[ \frac{y^2}{2} \right]_{y=0}^{y=\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}}$$
$$= x \left( \frac{(\sqrt{a^2 - x^2})^2}{2} - 0 \right) = x \left( \frac{a^2 - x^2}{2} \right) = \frac{1}{2} (a^2 x - x^3)$$

**Step 3: Evaluate the outer integral with respect to $x$**
$$I = \frac{1}{2} \int_{x=0}^a (a^2 x - x^3) \, dx$$
$$I = \frac{1}{2} \left[ a^2 \frac{x^2}{2} - \frac{x^4}{4} \right]_0^a$$

Substitute upper limit $x = a$ and lower limit $x = 0$:
$$I = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \left( a^2 \frac{a^2}{2} - \frac{a^4}{4} \right) - (0 - 0) \right]$$
$$I = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{a^4}{2} - \frac{a^4}{4} \right) = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{2a^4 - a^4}{4} \right) = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{a^4}{4} \right) = \frac{a^4}{8}$$

---

#### Approach 2: Polar Coordinates (Verification Method)
Substitute:
$$x = r\cos\theta, \quad y = r\sin\theta, \quad dx \, dy = r \, dr \, d\theta$$
Limits for the first quadrant:
$$0 \le r \le a, \qquad 0 \le \theta \le \frac{\pi}{2}$$

$$I = \int_{\theta=0}^{\pi/2} \int_{r=0}^a (r\cos\theta)(r\sin\theta) \cdot r \, dr \, d\theta$$
$$I = \left( \int_0^{\pi/2} \sin\theta \cos\theta \, d\theta \right) \cdot \left( \int_0^a r^3 \, dr \right)$$

1. Angle integral: $\int_0^{\pi/2} \sin\theta \cos\theta \, d\theta = \left[ \frac{\sin^2\theta}{2} \right]_0^{\pi/2} = \frac{1^2}{2} - 0 = \frac{1}{2}$.
2. Radial integral: $\int_0^a r^3 \, dr = \left[ \frac{r^4}{4} \right]_0^a = \frac{a^4}{4} - 0 = \frac{a^4}{4}$.

Multiply both parts:
$$I = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{a^4}{4} = \frac{a^4}{8}$$

Both methods match with $100\%$ precision!

---

#### ✅ Final Answer

Expand the following function in Fourier cosine series:

$$a_0 = \frac{2}{\pi} [x]_0^{\pi/2} = \frac{2}{\pi} \left( \frac{\pi}{2} - 0 \right) = \frac{2}{\pi} \cdot \frac{\pi}{2} = 1$$

$$\frac{a_0}{2} = \frac{1}{2}$$

### Question 16
Verify Cauchy-Riemann equation for the function:
$$f(z) = \frac{x - iy}{x^2 + y^2}$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Cauchy-Riemann Equations:** For an analytic function $f(z) = u(x, y) + i v(x, y)$:
   $$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}$$
2. **Quotient Rule for Partial Differentiation:**
   $$\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{U}{V}\right) = \frac{V \frac{\partial U}{\partial x} - U \frac{\partial V}{\partial x}}{V^2}$$

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Separate $f(z)$ into real part $u$ and imaginary part $v$**
$$f(z) = \frac{x - iy}{x^2 + y^2} = \frac{x}{x^2 + y^2} + i \left( \frac{-y}{x^2 + y^2} \right)$$
$$u(x, y) = \frac{x}{x^2 + y^2}, \qquad v(x, y) = \frac{-y}{x^2 + y^2}$$

**Step 2: Compute $\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}$**
Using the quotient rule with numerator $x$ and denominator $x^2 + y^2$:
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{(x^2 + y^2)\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x) - x\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^2 + y^2)}{(x^2 + y^2)^2}$$
$$= \frac{(x^2 + y^2)(1) - x(2x)}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{x^2 + y^2 - 2x^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{y^2 - x^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} \quad \text{--- (1)}$$

**Step 3: Compute $\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}$**
Here numerator $x$ is constant with respect to $y$:
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = \frac{(x^2 + y^2)(0) - x(2y)}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{-2xy}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} \quad \text{--- (2)}$$

**Step 4: Compute $\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}$**
Here numerator $-y$ is constant with respect to $x$:
$$\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = \frac{(x^2 + y^2)(0) - (-y)(2x)}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{2xy}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} \quad \text{--- (3)}$$

**Step 5: Compute $\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}$**
Using quotient rule on $v = \frac{-y}{x^2 + y^2}$:
$$\frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = \frac{(x^2 + y^2)\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(-y) - (-y)\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(x^2 + y^2)}{(x^2 + y^2)^2}$$
$$= \frac{(x^2 + y^2)(-1) - (-y)(2y)}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{-x^2 - y^2 + 2y^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{y^2 - x^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} \quad \text{--- (4)}$$

**Step 6: Compare and Verify**
- Comparing (1) and (4):
  $$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{y^2 - x^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} \quad \checkmark$$
- Comparing (2) and (3):
  $$\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = \frac{-2xy}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = -\left( \frac{2xy}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} \right) = -\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} \quad \checkmark$$

Both equations are identically satisfied for all $(x, y) \ne (0, 0)$.

### Question 19
State and prove the Cauchy-Riemann equations.

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Complex Derivative Limit Definition:**
   $$f'(z) = \lim_{\Delta z \to 0} \frac{f(z + \Delta z) - f(z)}{\Delta z}$$
   where $\Delta z = \Delta x + i \Delta y$.
2. **Independence of Path:** Because the complex limit must exist uniquely regardless of how $\Delta z \to 0$, taking limits horizontally ($\Delta y = 0$) and vertically ($\Delta x = 0$) must give the exact same value.

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Statement:**
Let $f(z) = u(x, y) + i v(x, y)$ be defined in an open region $D$. If $f(z)$ is differentiable at a point $z = x + iy \in D$, then the first-order partial derivatives $\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial v}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial v}{\partial y}$ all exist at $(x, y)$ and satisfy:
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}$$

**Proof:**
By definition of the derivative:
$$f'(z) = \lim_{\Delta z \to 0} \frac{f(z + \Delta z) - f(z)}{\Delta z}$$

Expressing in terms of real and imaginary parts:
$$f(z + \Delta z) - f(z) = [u(x + \Delta x, y + \Delta y) + i v(x + \Delta x, y + \Delta y)] - [u(x, y) + i v(x, y)]$$
$$= [u(x + \Delta x, y + \Delta y) - u(x, y)] + i [v(x + \Delta x, y + \Delta y) - v(x, y)]$$
and $\Delta z = \Delta x + i \Delta y$.

**Path 1: Let $\Delta z \to 0$ horizontally along the real axis ($\Delta y = 0 \implies \Delta z = \Delta x$):**
$$f'(z) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{[u(x + \Delta x, y) - u(x, y)] + i [v(x + \Delta x, y) - v(x, y)]}{\Delta x}$$
Splitting into real and imaginary limits:
$$f'(z) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{u(x + \Delta x, y) - u(x, y)}{\Delta x} + i \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{v(x + \Delta x, y) - v(x, y)}{\Delta x}$$
By definition of partial derivatives:
$$f'(z) = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + i \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)}$$

**Path 2: Let $\Delta z \to 0$ vertically along the imaginary axis ($\Delta x = 0 \implies \Delta z = i \Delta y$):**
$$f'(z) = \lim_{\Delta y \to 0} \frac{[u(x, y + \Delta y) - u(x, y)] + i [v(x, y + \Delta y) - v(x, y)]}{i \Delta y}$$
Divide through by $i$ (remembering $\frac{1}{i} = -i$):
$$f'(z) = \frac{1}{i} \lim_{\Delta y \to 0} \frac{u(x, y + \Delta y) - u(x, y)}{\Delta y} + \lim_{\Delta y \to 0} \frac{v(x, y + \Delta y) - v(x, y)}{\Delta y}$$
$$f'(z) = -i \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} - i \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} \quad \text{--- (Equation 2)}$$

**Equating the two expressions for $f'(z)$:**
Since $f(z)$ is differentiable at $z$, its derivative is unique:
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + i \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} - i \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}$$

Equating real parts:
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial y}$$

Equating imaginary parts:
$$\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = -\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} \iff \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}$$

This establishes the Cauchy-Riemann equations.

Find the Fourier integral representation of $f(x)$.

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed

   $$B(\lambda) = 0, \qquad A(\lambda) = 2 \int_0^\infty f(t) \cos(\lambda t) \, dt$$

---

Thus $f(x)$ is an **even function**.
Consequently:
$$B(\lambda) = 0$$

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_0^\infty \left( \frac{2 \sin \lambda}{\lambda} \right) \cos(\lambda x) \, d\lambda$$
$$f(x) = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^\infty \frac{\sin \lambda \cos(\lambda x)}{\lambda} \, d\lambda$$

*(Bonus Examination Insight: Setting $x = 0$ yields the famous Dirichlet integral $\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin \lambda}{\lambda} \, d\lambda = \frac{\pi}{2}$).*

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Equations Reducible to Homogeneous Form:**
   $$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{a_1 x + b_1 y + c_1}{a_2 x + b_2 y + c_2}$$
   When $\frac{a_1}{a_2} \ne \frac{b_1}{b_2}$, shift the origin to $(h, k)$ by setting $x = X + h, y = Y + k$.
2. **Homogeneous Substitution:** Once reduced to $\frac{dY}{dX} = g\left(\frac{Y}{X}\right)$, substitute $Y = vX \implies \frac{dY}{dX} = v + X \frac{dv}{dX}$.
3. **Partial Fractions:** Decompose rational expressions into integrable parts.

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Check the coefficients ratio**
- $a_1 = 2, b_1 = -1$
- $a_2 = 6, b_2 = -5$
$$\frac{a_1}{a_2} = \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}, \qquad \frac{b_1}{b_2} = \frac{-1}{-5} = \frac{1}{5}$$
Since $\frac{1}{3} \ne \frac{1}{5}$, we shift the origin.

**Step 2: Shift variables $x = X + h$ and $y = Y + k$**
Then $dx = dX$ and $dy = dY$:
$$\frac{dY}{dX} = \frac{2(X + h) - (Y + k) + 1}{6(X + h) - 5(Y + k) + 4} = \frac{2X - Y + (2h - k + 1)}{6X - 5Y + (6h - 5k + 4)}$$

Set the constant terms to zero:
1. $2h - k + 1 = 0 \implies k = 2h + 1$
2. $6h - 5k + 4 = 0$

Substitute $k = 2h + 1$ into equation 2:
$$6h - 5(2h + 1) + 4 = 0 \implies 6h - 10h - 5 + 4 = 0 \implies -4h - 1 = 0 \implies h = -\frac{1}{4}$$
$$k = 2\left(-\frac{1}{4}\right) + 1 = -\frac{1}{2} + 1 = \frac{1}{2}$$

Thus:
$$X = x + \frac{1}{4}, \qquad Y = y - \frac{1}{2}$$

The differential equation becomes homogeneous:
$$\frac{dY}{dX} = \frac{2X - Y}{6X - 5Y}$$

**Step 3: Apply the substitution $Y = vX$**
Then $\frac{dY}{dX} = v + X \frac{dv}{dX}$:
$$v + X \frac{dv}{dX} = \frac{2X - vX}{6X - 5vX} = \frac{2 - v}{6 - 5v}$$
$$X \frac{dv}{dX} = \frac{2 - v}{6 - 5v} - v = \frac{2 - v - v(6 - 5v)}{6 - 5v} = \frac{2 - v - 6v + 5v^2}{6 - 5v}$$
$$X \frac{dv}{dX} = \frac{5v^2 - 7v + 2}{6 - 5v}$$

**Step 4: Separate the variables**
$$\frac{6 - 5v}{5v^2 - 7v + 2} \, dv = \frac{dX}{X}$$

Factor the denominator:
$$5v^2 - 7v + 2 = 5v^2 - 5v - 2v + 2 = 5v(v - 1) - 2(v - 1) = (5v - 2)(v - 1)$$

Decompose into partial fractions:
$$\frac{6 - 5v}{(5v - 2)(v - 1)} = \frac{A}{5v - 2} + \frac{B}{v - 1}$$
$$6 - 5v = A(v - 1) + B(5v - 2)$$
- Put $v = 1$: $6 - 5 = B(5(1) - 2) \implies 1 = 3B \implies B = \frac{1}{3}$.
- Put $v = \frac{2}{5}$: $6 - 5\left(\frac{2}{5}\right) = A\left(\frac{2}{5} - 1\right) \implies 6 - 2 = A\left(-\frac{3}{5}\right) \implies 4 = -\frac{3}{5}A \implies A = -\frac{20}{3}$.

**Step 5: Integrate both sides**
$$\int \left( \frac{-20/3}{5v - 2} + \frac{1/3}{v - 1} \right) dv = \int \frac{dX}{X}$$
$$-\frac{20}{3} \cdot \frac{\ln|5v - 2|}{5} + \frac{1}{3} \ln|v - 1| = \ln|X| + C_1$$
$$-\frac{4}{3} \ln|5v - 2| + \frac{1}{3} \ln|v - 1| = \ln|X| + C_1$$

Multiply throughout by $3$:
$$\ln|v - 1| - 4\ln|5v - 2| = 3\ln|X| + 3C_1$$
$$\ln\left| \frac{v - 1}{(5v - 2)^4} \right| = \ln|X^3| + \ln C_2 = \ln|C_2 X^3|$$
$$\frac{v - 1}{(5v - 2)^4} = C_2 X^3$$

**Step 6: Substitute back $v = \frac{Y}{X}$**
$$\frac{\frac{Y}{X} - 1}{\left( \frac{5Y - 2X}{X} \right)^4} = C_2 X^3 \implies \frac{\frac{Y - X}{X}}{\frac{(5Y - 2X)^4}{X^4}} = \frac{(Y - X) X^3}{(5Y - 2X)^4} = C_2 X^3$$
Canceling $X^3$ from both sides:
$$\frac{Y - X}{(5Y - 2X)^4} = C_2 \implies Y - X = C_2 (5Y - 2X)^4$$

**Step 7: Substitute original variables $X = x + \frac{1}{4}$ and $Y = y - \frac{1}{2}$**
- $Y - X = \left(y - \frac{1}{2}\right) - \left(x + \frac{1}{4}\right) = y - x - \frac{3}{4} = \frac{4(y - x) - 3}{4}$
- $5Y - 2X = 5\left(y - \frac{1}{2}\right) - 2\left(x + \frac{1}{4}\right) = 5y - \frac{5}{2} - 2x - \frac{1}{2} = 5y - 2x - 3$

Substitute these back:
$$\frac{4(y - x) - 3}{4} = C_2 (5y - 2x - 3)^4$$
$$4(y - x) - 3 = C (5y - 2x - 3)^4 \quad (\text{where } C = 4C_2)$$

---

#### ✅ Final Answer
$$4(y - x) - 3 = C (5y - 2x - 3)^4$$

### Question 22
Find the general solution of the differential equation:
$$\frac{d^2 y}{dx^2} + 2 \frac{dy}{dx} + y = 2x + x^2$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Repeated Roots of Auxiliary Equation:** If $(m - m_1)^2 = 0$, then $y_c = (c_1 + c_2 x) e^{m_1 x}$.
2. **Particular Integral for Polynomial RHS via Negative Binomial Expansion:**
   $$\frac{1}{f(D)} P(x) = [f(D)]^{-1} P(x)$$
   Recall $(1 + D)^{-2} = 1 - 2D + 3D^2 - 4D^3 + \dots$

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Write in operator form**
$$(D^2 + 2D + 1)y = x^2 + 2x \implies (D + 1)^2 y = x^2 + 2x$$

**Step 2: Find the Complementary Function ($y_c$)**
Auxiliary equation:
$$m^2 + 2m + 1 = 0 \implies (m + 1)^2 = 0 \implies m = -1, -1 \quad \text{(repeated root)}$$

Thus:
$$y_c = (c_1 + c_2 x) e^{-x}$$

**Step 3: Find the Particular Integral ($y_p$)**
$$y_p = \frac{1}{(1 + D)^2} (x^2 + 2x) = (1 + D)^{-2} (x^2 + 2x)$$

Expand $(1 + D)^{-2}$ using the binomial theorem up to degree 2 (since the polynomial has degree 2):
$$(1 + D)^{-2} = 1 - 2D + 3D^2 - 4D^3 + \dots$$

Apply each operator term to $(x^2 + 2x)$:
1. **Term 1:** $1 \cdot (x^2 + 2x) = x^2 + 2x$
2. **Term 2:** $-2 D(x^2 + 2x) = -2 \frac{d}{dx}(x^2 + 2x) = -2(2x + 2) = -4x - 4$
3. **Term 3:** $3 D^2(x^2 + 2x) = 3 \frac{d^2}{dx^2}(x^2 + 2x) = 3 \frac{d}{dx}(2x + 2) = 3(2) = 6$
4. **Higher Terms:** $D^3(x^2 + 2x) = 0$ (all vanish).

**Step 4: Combine the terms of $y_p$**
$$y_p = (x^2 + 2x) + (-4x - 4) + 6$$
$$y_p = x^2 + (2x - 4x) + (-4 + 6) = x^2 - 2x + 2$$

**Step 5: Write the general solution**
$$y = y_c + y_p$$
$$y = (c_1 + c_2 x) e^{-x} + x^2 - 2x + 2$$

where $c_1$ and $c_2$ are arbitrary constants.

---

#### ✅ Final Answer
$$y = (c_1 + c_2 x) e^{-x} + x^2 - 2x + 2$$

### Question 23
Find the Fourier sine series for $f(x) = x^2$ in the interval $0 < x < 3$.

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Half-Range Sine Series on $(0, L)$:** With $L = 3$:
   $$f(x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{3}\right)$$
2. **Formula for Fourier Coefficient $b_n$:**
   $$b_n = \frac{2}{L} \int_0^L f(x) \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) dx = \frac{2}{3} \int_0^3 x^2 \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{3}\right) dx$$
3. **Bernoulli's Generalized Integration by Parts Formula:**
   $$\int u v \, dx = u v_1 - u' v_2 + u'' v_3 - u''' v_4 + \dots$$
   where primes indicate derivatives and subscripts indicate successive antiderivatives.

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Set up successive derivatives and integrals**
Let $k = \frac{n\pi}{3}$.
- $u = x^2 \implies u' = 2x \implies u'' = 2 \implies u''' = 0$
- $v = \sin(kx)$
  - $v_1 = \int \sin(kx) \, dx = -\frac{\cos(kx)}{k}$
  - $v_2 = \int v_1 \, dx = -\frac{\sin(kx)}{k^2}$
  - $v_3 = \int v_2 \, dx = \frac{\cos(kx)}{k^3}$

**Step 2: Apply Bernoulli's Formula**
$$\int_0^3 x^2 \sin(kx) \, dx = \left[ x^2 \left(-\frac{\cos(kx)}{k}\right) - (2x) \left(-\frac{\sin(kx)}{k^2}\right) + (2) \left(\frac{\cos(kx)}{k^3}\right) \right]_0^3$$
$$= \left[ -\frac{x^2 \cos(kx)}{k} + \frac{2x \sin(kx)}{k^2} + \frac{2\cos(kx)}{k^3} \right]_0^3$$

**Step 3: Evaluate at the limits**
At $x = 3$: $k \cdot 3 = \frac{n\pi}{3} \cdot 3 = n\pi$.
- $\cos(n\pi) = (-1)^n$
- $\sin(n\pi) = 0$

$$\text{Value at } 3 = -\frac{(3)^2 \cos(n\pi)}{k} + \frac{2(3) \sin(n\pi)}{k^2} + \frac{2\cos(n\pi)}{k^3}$$
$$= -\frac{9(-1)^n}{k} + 0 + \frac{2(-1)^n}{k^3} = -\frac{9(-1)^n}{k} + \frac{2(-1)^n}{k^3}$$

At $x = 0$:
$$\text{Value at } 0 = 0 + 0 + \frac{2\cos(0)}{k^3} = \frac{2(1)}{k^3} = \frac{2}{k^3}$$

Subtracting:
$$\int_0^3 x^2 \sin(kx) \, dx = \left(-\frac{9(-1)^n}{k} + \frac{2(-1)^n}{k^3}\right) - \frac{2}{k^3} = -\frac{9(-1)^n}{k} + \frac{2((-1)^n - 1)}{k^3}$$

**Step 4: Substitute $k = \frac{n\pi}{3}$**
- $\frac{1}{k} = \frac{3}{n\pi} \implies -\frac{9}{k} = -\frac{27}{n\pi}$
- $\frac{1}{k^3} = \frac{27}{n^3\pi^3} \implies \frac{2}{k^3} = \frac{54}{n^3\pi^3}$

Thus:
$$\int_0^3 x^2 \sin(kx) \, dx = -\frac{27(-1)^n}{n\pi} + \frac{54((-1)^n - 1)}{n^3\pi^3}$$

**Step 5: Multiply by $\frac{2}{3}$ to get $b_n$**
$$b_n = \frac{2}{3} \left[ -\frac{27(-1)^n}{n\pi} + \frac{54((-1)^n - 1)}{n^3\pi^3} \right]$$
$$b_n = -\frac{18(-1)^n}{n\pi} + \frac{36((-1)^n - 1)}{n^3\pi^3} = \frac{18(-1)^{n+1}}{n\pi} - \frac{36(1 - (-1)^n)}{n^3\pi^3}$$

- When $n$ is even ($n = 2m$): $(-1)^n = 1 \implies b_{2m} = -\frac{18}{2m\pi} = -\frac{9}{m\pi}$
- When $n$ is odd ($n = 2m - 1$): $(-1)^n = -1 \implies b_n = \frac{18}{n\pi} - \frac{72}{n^3\pi^3}$

**Step 6: Write down the Fourier sine series**
$$f(x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \left[ \frac{18(-1)^{n+1}}{n\pi} - \frac{36(1 - (-1)^n)}{n^3\pi^3} \right] \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{3}\right)$$

### Question 24
Find an analytic function $f(z)$ whose real part is $e^x (x \cos y - y \sin y)$.

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Milne-Thomson Method:**
   Given the real part $u(x, y)$ of an analytic function $f(z) = u + iv$:
   $$f'(z) = u_x(z, 0) - i u_y(z, 0)$$
   where $u_x = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}$ and $u_y = \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}$.
2. Integrating $f'(z)$ directly with respect to $z$ yields $f(z) = \int f'(z) \, dz + C$.

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

**Step 1: Given real part**
$$u(x, y) = e^x (x \cos y - y \sin y)$$

**Step 2: Differentiate $u$ partially with respect to $x$**
Apply the product rule: $\frac{\partial}{\partial x}[f \cdot g] = f' g + f g'$:
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(e^x) \cdot (x \cos y - y \sin y) + e^x \cdot \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x \cos y - y \sin y)$$
$$= e^x (x \cos y - y \sin y) + e^x (\cos y)$$
$$= e^x [(x + 1)\cos y - y \sin y]$$

**Step 3: Differentiate $u$ partially with respect to $y$**
Treat $x$ as constant:
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = e^x \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(x \cos y - y \sin y)$$
$$= e^x \left[ x(-\sin y) - \left( (1)\sin y + y \cos y \right) \right]$$
$$= e^x [ -x \sin y - \sin y - y \cos y ] = -e^x [ (x + 1)\sin y + y \cos y ]$$

**Step 4: Apply the Milne-Thomson substitution ($x = z, y = 0$)**
Recall $\cos 0 = 1$ and $\sin 0 = 0$:
$$u_x(z, 0) = e^z [(z + 1)(1) - 0(0)] = (z + 1) e^z$$
$$u_y(z, 0) = -e^z [(z + 1)(0) + 0(1)] = 0$$

**Step 5: Form $f'(z)$**
$$f'(z) = u_x(z, 0) - i u_y(z, 0) = (z + 1) e^z - i(0) = (z + 1) e^z$$

**Step 6: Integrate $f'(z)$ with respect to $z$**
$$f(z) = \int (z + 1) e^z \, dz + C = \int z e^z \, dz + \int e^z \, dz + C$$

Using integration by parts on $\int z e^z \, dz = z e^z - e^z$:
$$f(z) = (z e^z - e^z) + e^z + C = z e^z + C$$

**Step 7: Verification (Sanity Check)**
Let $z = x + iy$:
$$f(z) = (x + iy) e^{x + iy} = (x + iy) e^x (\cos y + i \sin y)$$
$$= e^x [ x\cos y + i x \sin y + i y \cos y + i^2 y \sin y ]$$
$$= e^x [ (x \cos y - y \sin y) + i (x \sin y + y \cos y) ]$$
The real part is $e^x(x\cos y - y\sin y)$, exactly matching $u(x, y)$!

---

#### ✅ Final Answer
$$f(z) = z e^z + C$$

### Question 25
Find the residue of:
$$f(z) = \frac{z}{(z - 1)(z - 2)^2} \quad \text{at its poles.}$$

#### 💡 Core Theory & Formulas Needed
1. **Pole Identification:** The poles are the roots of the denominator $(z - 1)(z - 2)^2 = 0$:
   - $z = 1$ is a simple pole (order $m = 1$).
   - $z = 2$ is a pole of order 2 (double pole, $m = 2$).
2. **Residue at a Simple Pole ($m = 1$):**
   $$\text{Res}(f, z_0) = \lim_{z \to z_0} (z - z_0) f(z)$$
3. **Residue at a Multiple Pole of Order $m$:**
   $$\text{Res}(f, z_0) = \frac{1}{(m - 1)!} \lim_{z \to z_0} \frac{d^{m-1}}{dz^{m-1}} \left[ (z - z_0)^m f(z) \right]$$

---

#### 📝 Step-by-Step Complete Solution

#### Part 1: Residue at simple pole $z = 1$
$$\text{Res}(f, 1) = \lim_{z \to 1} (z - 1) f(z) = \lim_{z \to 1} \left[ (z - 1) \cdot \frac{z}{(z - 1)(z - 2)^2} \right]$$

Cancel the common factor $(z - 1)$:
$$= \lim_{z \to 1} \frac{z}{(z - 2)^2}$$

Substitute $z = 1$:
$$\text{Res}(f, 1) = \frac{1}{(1 - 2)^2} = \frac{1}{(-1)^2} = \frac{1}{1} = 1$$

---

#### Part 2: Residue at pole of order 2 at $z = 2$
For $m = 2$ and $z_0 = 2$:
$$\text{Res}(f, 2) = \frac{1}{(2 - 1)!} \lim_{z \to 2} \frac{d}{dz} \left[ (z - 2)^2 f(z) \right]$$
$$= \frac{1}{1!} \lim_{z \to 2} \frac{d}{dz} \left[ (z - 2)^2 \cdot \frac{z}{(z - 1)(z - 2)^2} \right]$$

Cancel $(z - 2)^2$:
$$= \lim_{z \to 2} \frac{d}{dz} \left( \frac{z}{z - 1} \right)$$

Differentiate $\frac{z}{z - 1}$ using the quotient rule:
$$\frac{d}{dz}\left(\frac{z}{z - 1}\right) = \frac{(z - 1)\frac{d}{dz}(z) - z \frac{d}{dz}(z - 1)}{(z - 1)^2} = \frac{(z - 1)(1) - z(1)}{(z - 1)^2} = \frac{z - 1 - z}{(z - 1)^2} = \frac{-1}{(z - 1)^2}$$

Now take the limit as $z \to 2$:
$$\text{Res}(f, 2) = \lim_{z \to 2} \frac{-1}{(z - 1)^2} = \frac{-1}{(2 - 1)^2} = \frac{-1}{1^2} = -1$$

---

#### Part 3: Verification (Sum of Residues Theorem)
The sum of all residues in the finite complex plane is:
$$\sum \text{Res} = \text{Res}(f, 1) + \text{Res}(f, 2) = 1 + (-1) = 0$$
Since the degree of the denominator (3) exceeds the degree of the numerator (1) by at least 2, the residue at infinity is $\text{Res}(f, \infty) = 0$. By Cauchy's Residue Theorem on the Riemann sphere:
$$\sum_{\text{all poles}} \text{Res} = 0 \implies 1 + (-1) = 0 \quad \checkmark$$

---

#### ✅ Final Answer
- Residue at $z = 1$: $\mathbf{1}$
- Residue at $z = 2$: $\mathbf{-1}$
