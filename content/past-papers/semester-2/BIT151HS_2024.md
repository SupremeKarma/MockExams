### 1. Evaluate:
$$\int_0^a \int_0^a \int_0^a (x + y + z) \, dx \, dy \, dz$$

**Solution:**
Let the given triple integral be:
$$I = \int_0^a \int_0^a \int_0^a (x + y + z) \, dx \, dy \, dz$$

Since the region of integration is a symmetric cube $[0, a] \times [0, a] \times [0, a]$, each coordinate $x, y, z$ contributes identically by symmetry:
$$\iiint_V x \, dx \, dy \, dz = \iiint_V y \, dx \, dy \, dz = \iiint_V z \, dx \, dy \, dz$$

Evaluating one such term:
$$\int_0^a \int_0^a \int_0^a x \, dx \, dy \, dz = \left( \int_0^a x \, dx \right) \left( \int_0^a dy \right) \left( \int_0^a dz \right) = \left[ \frac{x^2}{2} \right]_0^a \cdot [y]_0^a \cdot [z]_0^a = \frac{a^2}{2} \cdot a \cdot a = \frac{a^4}{2}$$

By linearity:
$$I = 3 \times \frac{a^4}{2} = \frac{3a^4}{2}$$

*Direct step-by-step verification:*
1. Inner integral with respect to $x$:
   $$\int_0^a (x + y + z) \, dx = \left[ \frac{x^2}{2} + (y + z)x \right]_0^a = \frac{a^2}{2} + a(y + z)$$
2. Middle integral with respect to $y$:
   $$\int_0^a \left( \frac{a^2}{2} + ay + az \right) \, dy = \left[ \frac{a^2 y}{2} + \frac{a y^2}{2} + azy \right]_0^a = \frac{a^3}{2} + \frac{a^3}{2} + a^2 z = a^3 + a^2 z$$
3. Outer integral with respect to $z$:
   $$\int_0^a (a^3 + a^2 z) \, dz = \left[ a^3 z + \frac{a^2 z^2}{2} \right]_0^a = a^4 + \frac{a^4}{2} = \frac{3a^4}{2}$$

$$\therefore \int_0^a \int_0^a \int_0^a (x + y + z) \, dx \, dy \, dz = \frac{3a^4}{2}$$

### 2. Evaluate the integral by changing the order of integration:
$$\int_0^\infty \int_x^\infty \frac{e^{-y}}{y} \, dy \, dx$$

**Solution:**
Let the given integral be:
$$I = \int_{x=0}^\infty \int_{y=x}^\infty \frac{e^{-y}}{y} \, dy \, dx$$

**Region of Integration:**
- Inner limits: $y = x$ to $y = \infty$
- Outer limits: $x = 0$ to $x = \infty$

This region $R$ in the first quadrant of the $xy$-plane is bounded by the line $y = x$ and the $y$-axis ($x = 0$), extending to $\infty$ upwards.

**Changing the Order of Integration:**
To integrate first with respect to $x$ and then with respect to $y$:
- For a fixed $y$, horizontal strips run from the $y$-axis ($x = 0$) to the line $x = y$.
- $y$ varies from $0$ to $\infty$.

Thus, the integral becomes:
$$I = \int_{y=0}^\infty \int_{x=0}^y \frac{e^{-y}}{y} \, dx \, dy$$

Evaluating the inner integral with respect to $x$:
$$\int_{x=0}^y \frac{e^{-y}}{y} \, dx = \frac{e^{-y}}{y} [x]_0^y = \frac{e^{-y}}{y} (y - 0) = e^{-y}$$

Now, evaluating the outer integral with respect to $y$:
$$I = \int_0^\infty e^{-y} \, dy = \left[ -e^{-y} \right]_0^\infty = -(0 - 1) = 1$$

$$\therefore \int_0^\infty \int_x^\infty \frac{e^{-y}}{y} \, dy \, dx = 1$$

### 3. Determine the order and degree of the differential equation:
$$\sqrt{\frac{d^3 y}{dx^3}} = \frac{dy}{dx}$$

**Solution:**
Given differential equation:
$$\sqrt{\frac{d^3 y}{dx^3}} = \frac{dy}{dx}$$

To determine the order and degree, the differential equation must be expressed in a form free from radicals and fraction exponents on the derivatives.

Squaring both sides:
$$\frac{d^3 y}{dx^3} = \left( \frac{dy}{dx} \right)^2$$

1. **Order:** The highest derivative present in the equation is $\frac{d^3 y}{dx^3}$, which is of order **3**.
2. **Degree:** When cleared of fractional powers, the highest derivative $\frac{d^3 y}{dx^3}$ is raised to the power **1**.

$$\therefore \text{Order} = 3, \quad \text{Degree} = 1$$

### 4. Solve:
$$(x + 1) \, dy = (y - 1) \, dx$$

**Solution:**
Given first-order differential equation:
$$(x + 1) \, dy = (y - 1) \, dx$$

Separating the variables:
$$\frac{dy}{y - 1} = \frac{dx}{x + 1}$$

Integrating both sides:
$$\int \frac{dy}{y - 1} = \int \frac{dx}{x + 1} + \ln C$$
$$\ln |y - 1| = \ln |x + 1| + \ln C$$
$$\ln |y - 1| = \ln |C (x + 1)|$$

Taking the exponential of both sides:
$$y - 1 = C (x + 1)$$
$$y = C (x + 1) + 1$$

where $C$ is an arbitrary constant of integration.

### 8. If $f(z)$ is differentiable at $z_0$, then show that $f(z)$ is continuous at $z = z_0$.

**Solution:**
**Given:** $f(z)$ is differentiable at $z = z_0$, which means:
$$f'(z_0) = \lim_{z \to z_0} \frac{f(z) - f(z_0)}{z - z_0} \quad \text{exists finitely.}$$

**To Prove:** $f(z)$ is continuous at $z = z_0$, i.e., $\lim_{z \to z_0} f(z) = f(z_0)$, or equivalently $\lim_{z \to z_0} [f(z) - f(z_0)] = 0$.

**Proof:**
For any $z \ne z_0$, we can write:
$$f(z) - f(z_0) = \frac{f(z) - f(z_0)}{z - z_0} \cdot (z - z_0)$$

Taking the limit as $z \to z_0$ on both sides:
$$\lim_{z \to z_0} [f(z) - f(z_0)] = \lim_{z \to z_0} \left[ \frac{f(z) - f(z_0)}{z - z_0} \cdot (z - z_0) \right]$$

Applying product rule for limits:
$$\lim_{z \to z_0} [f(z) - f(z_0)] = \left( \lim_{z \to z_0} \frac{f(z) - f(z_0)}{z - z_0} \right) \cdot \left( \lim_{z \to z_0} (z - z_0) \right)$$
$$\lim_{z \to z_0} [f(z) - f(z_0)] = f'(z_0) \cdot 0 = 0$$

Therefore:
$$\lim_{z \to z_0} f(z) = f(z_0)$$

Hence, $f(z)$ is continuous at $z = z_0$.

### 10. Show that the function:
$$f(z) = \frac{z^2 - 2z + 5}{z - 2} \quad \text{has a simple pole at } z = 2.$$

**Solution:**
A point $z = z_0$ is a pole of order $m$ of $f(z)$ if $\lim_{z \to z_0} (z - z_0)^m f(z)$ is a non-zero finite constant.

For $z_0 = 2$ and $m = 1$:
$$\lim_{z \to 2} (z - 2) f(z) = \lim_{z \to 2} (z - 2) \cdot \frac{z^2 - 2z + 5}{z - 2} = \lim_{z \to 2} (z^2 - 2z + 5)$$

Evaluating the limit:
$$= (2)^2 - 2(2) + 5 = 4 - 4 + 5 = 5$$

Since the limit is finite and non-zero ($5 \ne 0$) for $m = 1$, the singularity at $z = 2$ is a pole of order $1$, which is a **simple pole**.

$$\therefore f(z) \text{ has a simple pole at } z = 2 \text{ with residue } \text{Res}(f, 2) = 5.$$

### 11. Evaluate:
$$\iint_R xy \, dx \, dy \quad \text{where } R \text{ is the positive quadrant of the circle } x^2 + y^2 = a^2.$$

**Solution:**
**Method 1: Cartesian Coordinates**
The region $R$ is bounded by $x = 0$, $y = 0$, and the circular arc $x^2 + y^2 = a^2$ in the first quadrant:
- $x$ varies from $0$ to $a$
- For a given $x$, $y$ varies from $0$ to $\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$

$$I = \int_0^a \left[ \int_0^{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}} xy \, dy \right] dx = \int_0^a x \left[ \frac{y^2}{2} \right]_0^{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}} dx$$
$$I = \frac{1}{2} \int_0^a x (a^2 - x^2) \, dx = \frac{1}{2} \int_0^a (a^2 x - x^3) \, dx$$
$$I = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \frac{a^2 x^2}{2} - \frac{x^4}{4} \right]_0^a = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{a^4}{2} - \frac{a^4}{4} \right) = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{a^4}{4} \right) = \frac{a^4}{8}$$

**Method 2: Polar Coordinates (Alternative Verification)**
Substitute $x = r \cos\theta$, $y = r \sin\theta$, with Jacobian $dx \, dy = r \, dr \, d\theta$.
In the first quadrant:
- $r$ runs from $0$ to $a$
- $\theta$ runs from $0$ to $\frac{\pi}{2}$

$$I = \int_0^{\pi/2} \int_0^a (r \cos\theta)(r \sin\theta) \cdot r \, dr \, d\theta = \left( \int_0^{\pi/2} \sin\theta \cos\theta \, d\theta \right) \left( \int_0^a r^3 \, dr \right)$$
$$= \left[ \frac{\sin^2\theta}{2} \right]_0^{\pi/2} \cdot \left[ \frac{r^4}{4} \right]_0^a = \left( \frac{1}{2} - 0 \right) \cdot \left( \frac{a^4}{4} - 0 \right) = \frac{a^4}{8}$$

$$\therefore \iint_R xy \, dx \, dy = \frac{a^4}{8}$$

### 12. Solve:
$$\frac{dy}{dx} + \frac{y}{x^2} = \frac{1}{x^2}$$

**Solution:**
The given equation is a first-order linear differential equation of standard form:
$$\frac{dy}{dx} + P(x) y = Q(x)$$
where:
$$P(x) = \frac{1}{x^2} = x^{-2}, \quad Q(x) = \frac{1}{x^2} = x^{-2}$$

**Step 1: Find the Integrating Factor (I.F.):**
$$\text{I.F.} = e^{\int P(x) \, dx} = e^{\int x^{-2} \, dx} = e^{-1/x}$$

**Step 2: General Solution Formula:**
$$y \cdot (\text{I.F.}) = \int Q(x) \cdot (\text{I.F.}) \, dx + C$$
$$y e^{-1/x} = \int \frac{1}{x^2} e^{-1/x} \, dx + C$$

**Step 3: Evaluate the Integral:**
Let $t = -\frac{1}{x} \implies dt = \frac{1}{x^2} \, dx$.
$$\int \frac{1}{x^2} e^{-1/x} \, dx = \int e^t \, dt = e^t = e^{-1/x}$$

Substituting back:
$$y e^{-1/x} = e^{-1/x} + C$$

Multiplying throughout by $e^{1/x}$:
$$y = 1 + C e^{1/x}$$

where $C$ is an arbitrary constant.

### 13. Find the general solution of the differential equation:
$$\frac{d^2 y}{dx^2} - \frac{dy}{dx} - 2y = 6e^x$$

**Solution:**
In operator notation with $D = \frac{d}{dx}$:
$$(D^2 - D - 2)y = 6e^x$$

**Step 1: Complementary Function ($y_c$):**
The auxiliary equation is:
$$m^2 - m - 2 = 0 \implies (m - 2)(m + 1) = 0 \implies m_1 = 2, \quad m_2 = -1$$

Since the roots are real and distinct:
$$y_c = c_1 e^{2x} + c_2 e^{-x}$$

**Step 2: Particular Integral ($y_p$):**
$$y_p = \frac{1}{D^2 - D - 2} (6e^x)$$

For $f(D) = D^2 - D - 2$ with RHS of form $e^{ax}$ where $a = 1$:
$$f(1) = (1)^2 - (1) - 2 = 1 - 1 - 2 = -2 \ne 0$$

Therefore:
$$y_p = \frac{6e^x}{f(1)} = \frac{6e^x}{-2} = -3e^x$$

**Step 3: General Solution:**
$$y = y_c + y_p = c_1 e^{2x} + c_2 e^{-x} - 3e^x$$

where $c_1, c_2$ are arbitrary constants.

### 14. Find the Fourier series for the function $f(x) = 2x, 0 \le x \le \pi$ where $f(x) = f(x + 2\pi)$.

**Solution:**
When $f(x) = 2x$ is extended periodically with period $2\pi$:
If treated over the standard symmetric interval $(-\pi, \pi)$ as an odd periodic function $f(-x) = -f(x)$:
1. $a_0 = 0$
2. $a_n = 0$ for all $n \ge 1$

The Fourier sine coefficients are:
$$b_n = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^\pi f(x) \sin(nx) \, dx = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^\pi 2x \sin(nx) \, dx = \frac{4}{\pi} \int_0^\pi x \sin(nx) \, dx$$

Integrating by parts:
$$\int_0^\pi x \sin(nx) \, dx = \left[ -x \frac{\cos(nx)}{n} \right]_0^\pi - \int_0^\pi \left( -\frac{\cos(nx)}{n} \right) dx$$
$$= \left( -\frac{\pi \cos(n\pi)}{n} - 0 \right) + \left[ \frac{\sin(nx)}{n^2} \right]_0^\pi = -\frac{\pi (-1)^n}{n} + 0 = \frac{\pi (-1)^{n+1}}{n}$$

Therefore:
$$b_n = \frac{4}{\pi} \cdot \frac{\pi (-1)^{n+1}}{n} = \frac{4(-1)^{n+1}}{n}$$

The Fourier series is:
$$f(x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n \sin(nx) = 4 \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n} \sin(nx)$$
$$f(x) = 4 \left( \sin x - \frac{1}{2} \sin 2x + \frac{1}{3} \sin 3x - \frac{1}{4} \sin 4x + \dots \right)$$

*(Note: If considered as full period on $(0, 2\pi)$ with $f(x) = 2x$: $a_0 = 4\pi, a_n = 0, b_n = -\frac{4}{n} \implies f(x) = 2\pi - 4\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{\sin nx}{n}$. Both forms reflect the periodic extension).*

### 15. Expand the following function in Fourier cosine series:
$$f(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & 0 < x < \frac{\pi}{2} \\ 0, & \frac{\pi}{2} < x < \pi \end{cases}$$

**Solution:**
The Fourier cosine series of a function $f(x)$ on $(0, \pi)$ is given by:
$$f(x) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n \cos(nx)$$

**Step 1: Compute $a_0$:**
$$a_0 = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^\pi f(x) \, dx = \frac{2}{\pi} \left[ \int_0^{\pi/2} 1 \, dx + \int_{\pi/2}^\pi 0 \, dx \right] = \frac{2}{\pi} [x]_0^{\pi/2} = \frac{2}{\pi} \cdot \frac{\pi}{2} = 1$$
Thus:
$$\frac{a_0}{2} = \frac{1}{2}$$

**Step 2: Compute $a_n$:**
$$a_n = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^\pi f(x) \cos(nx) \, dx = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^{\pi/2} 1 \cdot \cos(nx) \, dx = \frac{2}{\pi} \left[ \frac{\sin(nx)}{n} \right]_0^{\pi/2}$$
$$a_n = \frac{2}{n\pi} \sin\left(\frac{n\pi}{2}\right)$$

Analyzing for different values of $n$:
- For even $n = 2k$: $\sin(k\pi) = 0 \implies a_{2k} = 0$.
- For odd $n = 2k - 1$:
  - $n = 1: a_1 = \frac{2}{\pi} \sin(\frac{\pi}{2}) = \frac{2}{\pi}$
  - $n = 3: a_3 = \frac{2}{3\pi} \sin(\frac{3\pi}{2}) = -\frac{2}{3\pi}$
  - $n = 5: a_5 = \frac{2}{5\pi} \sin(\frac{5\pi}{2}) = \frac{2}{5\pi}$
  - In general, $a_{2k-1} = \frac{2(-1)^{k-1}}{(2k - 1)\pi}$.

**Step 3: Fourier Cosine Series:**
$$f(x) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{2}{\pi} \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{2k - 1} \cos((2k - 1)x)$$
$$f(x) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{2}{\pi} \left( \cos x - \frac{1}{3} \cos 3x + \frac{1}{5} \cos 5x - \frac{1}{7} \cos 7x + \dots \right)$$

### 16. Verify Cauchy-Riemann equation for the function:
$$f(z) = \frac{x - iy}{x^2 + y^2}$$

**Solution:**
Given:
$$f(z) = \frac{x - iy}{x^2 + y^2} = u(x, y) + i v(x, y)$$

where:
$$u(x, y) = \frac{x}{x^2 + y^2}, \quad v(x, y) = \frac{-y}{x^2 + y^2}$$

*(Note: For $z = x + iy \ne 0$, $f(z) = \frac{\bar{z}}{|z|^2} = \frac{1}{z}$).*

**Step 1: Compute partial derivatives of $u(x, y)$:**
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{(1)(x^2 + y^2) - x(2x)}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{y^2 - x^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2}$$
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = \frac{(0)(x^2 + y^2) - x(2y)}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{-2xy}{(x^2 + y^2)^2}$$

**Step 2: Compute partial derivatives of $v(x, y)$:**
$$\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = \frac{(0)(x^2 + y^2) - (-y)(2x)}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{2xy}{(x^2 + y^2)^2}$$
$$\frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = \frac{(-1)(x^2 + y^2) - (-y)(2y)}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{-x^2 - y^2 + 2y^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{y^2 - x^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2}$$

**Step 3: Verification:**
1. $\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{y^2 - x^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial y}$
2. $\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = \frac{-2xy}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = -\left( \frac{2xy}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} \right) = -\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}$

Both Cauchy-Riemann equations:
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}$$
are identically satisfied for all $(x, y) \ne (0, 0)$.

### 17. Expand $f(z) = \frac{1}{z}$ by Taylor's series about the point $z = 1$.

**Solution:**
The Taylor series expansion of an analytic function $f(z)$ about $z = z_0 = 1$ is:
$$f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{f^{(n)}(1)}{n!} (z - 1)^n$$

**Method 1: By Successive Differentiation**
- $f(z) = z^{-1} \implies f(1) = 1$
- $f'(z) = -z^{-2} \implies f'(1) = -1$
- $f''(z) = 2z^{-3} \implies f''(1) = 2$
- $f'''(z) = -6z^{-4} \implies f'''(1) = -6 = -3!$
- In general: $f^{(n)}(z) = (-1)^n n! z^{-(n+1)} \implies f^{(n)}(1) = (-1)^n n!$

Hence:
$$\frac{f^{(n)}(1)}{n!} = \frac{(-1)^n n!}{n!} = (-1)^n$$

Substituting into the Taylor series:
$$f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty (-1)^n (z - 1)^n = 1 - (z - 1) + (z - 1)^2 - (z - 1)^3 + \dots$$

**Method 2: By Binomial Geometric Series**
$$f(z) = \frac{1}{z} = \frac{1}{1 + (z - 1)} = [1 + (z - 1)]^{-1}$$

For $|z - 1| < 1$, expanding using geometric series:
$$[1 + (z - 1)]^{-1} = 1 - (z - 1) + (z - 1)^2 - (z - 1)^3 + \dots = \sum_{n=0}^\infty (-1)^n (z - 1)^n$$

**Region of Convergence:**
The distance from the expansion center $z_0 = 1$ to the nearest singularity ($z = 0$) is $R = |1 - 0| = 1$.
$$\therefore \frac{1}{z} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty (-1)^n (z - 1)^n \quad \text{valid for } |z - 1| < 1.$$

### 18. Solve the equation:
$$x \frac{dy}{dx} = y + x^2 \log x$$

**Solution:**
Given differential equation:
$$x \frac{dy}{dx} = y + x^2 \log x$$

Rearranging in standard linear form (dividing by $x$ for $x > 0$):
$$\frac{dy}{dx} - \frac{1}{x} y = x \log x$$

This is a first-order linear differential equation:
$$\frac{dy}{dx} + P(x) y = Q(x)$$
with $P(x) = -\frac{1}{x}$ and $Q(x) = x \log x$.

**Step 1: Integrating Factor (I.F.):**
$$\text{I.F.} = e^{\int P(x) \, dx} = e^{-\int \frac{1}{x} \, dx} = e^{-\ln x} = e^{\ln(x^{-1})} = \frac{1}{x}$$

**Step 2: General Solution:**
$$y \cdot (\text{I.F.}) = \int Q(x) \cdot (\text{I.F.}) \, dx + C$$
$$y \cdot \frac{1}{x} = \int (x \log x) \cdot \frac{1}{x} \, dx + C$$
$$\frac{y}{x} = \int \log x \, dx + C$$

**Step 3: Evaluate $\int \log x \, dx$:**
Using integration by parts ($\int u \, dv = uv - \int v \, du$ with $u = \log x, dv = dx$):
$$\int \log x \, dx = x \log x - \int x \cdot \frac{1}{x} \, dx = x \log x - x$$

Substituting:
$$\frac{y}{x} = x \log x - x + C$$

### 19. State and prove the Cauchy-Riemann equations.

**Solution:**
**Statement:**
Let $f(z) = u(x, y) + i v(x, y)$ be defined in a domain $D$. If $f(z)$ is differentiable at a point $z = x + iy \in D$, then the first-order partial derivatives of $u$ and $v$ exist at $(x, y)$ and satisfy the Cauchy-Riemann equations:
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}$$

**Proof:**
Since $f(z)$ is differentiable at $z$, by definition:
$$f'(z) = \lim_{\Delta z \to 0} \frac{f(z + \Delta z) - f(z)}{\Delta z}$$
exists and is unique regardless of the path along which $\Delta z \to 0$.

Here $z = x + iy$ and $\Delta z = \Delta x + i \Delta y$:
$$f(z + \Delta z) - f(z) = [u(x + \Delta x, y + \Delta y) - u(x, y)] + i [v(x + \Delta x, y + \Delta y) - v(x, y)]$$

**Case 1: Let $\Delta z \to 0$ horizontally along the real axis ($\Delta y = 0, \Delta z = \Delta x$):**
$$f'(z) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{[u(x + \Delta x, y) - u(x, y)] + i [v(x + \Delta x, y) - v(x, y)]}{\Delta x}$$
$$f'(z) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{u(x + \Delta x, y) - u(x, y)}{\Delta x} + i \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{v(x + \Delta x, y) - v(x, y)}{\Delta x}$$
$$f'(z) = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + i \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} \quad \text{--- (1)}$$

**Case 2: Let $\Delta z \to 0$ vertically along the imaginary axis ($\Delta x = 0, \Delta z = i \Delta y$):**
$$f'(z) = \lim_{\Delta y \to 0} \frac{[u(x, y + \Delta y) - u(x, y)] + i [v(x, y + \Delta y) - v(x, y)]}{i \Delta y}$$
$$f'(z) = \frac{1}{i} \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = -i \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y}$$
$$f'(z) = \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} - i \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} \quad \text{--- (2)}$$

**Equating (1) and (2):**
Since $f'(z)$ is unique:
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + i \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} - i \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}$$

Equating real and imaginary parts:
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = -\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} \implies \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}$$

This completes the proof.

### 20. A function $f(x)$ is defined by:
$$f(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{for } -1 \le x \le 1 \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$
Find the Fourier integral representation of $f(x)$.

**Solution:**
The Fourier integral representation of a function $f(x)$ is:
$$f(x) = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_0^\infty [A(\lambda) \cos(\lambda x) + B(\lambda) \sin(\lambda x)] \, d\lambda$$
where:
$$A(\lambda) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(t) \cos(\lambda t) \, dt, \quad B(\lambda) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(t) \sin(\lambda t) \, dt$$

**Symmetry Inspection:**
Since $f(-x) = f(x)$, $f(x)$ is an **even function**.
- The sine coefficient vanishes: $B(\lambda) = 0$.
- The cosine coefficient simplifies to:
  $$A(\lambda) = 2 \int_0^\infty f(t) \cos(\lambda t) \, dt$$

**Compute $A(\lambda)$:**
Given $f(t) = 1$ for $0 \le t \le 1$ and $f(t) = 0$ for $t > 1$:
$$A(\lambda) = 2 \int_0^1 1 \cdot \cos(\lambda t) \, dt = 2 \left[ \frac{\sin(\lambda t)}{\lambda} \right]_0^1 = 2 \frac{\sin \lambda}{\lambda}$$

**Substitute into Fourier Integral:**
$$f(x) = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_0^\infty \left( 2 \frac{\sin \lambda}{\lambda} \right) \cos(\lambda x) \, d\lambda$$
$$f(x) = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^\infty \frac{\sin \lambda \cos(\lambda x)}{\lambda} \, d\lambda$$

*(Deduction: By Fourier Integral Theorem, this integral equals $1$ for $|x| < 1$, $\frac{1}{2}$ at $x = \pm 1$, and $0$ for $|x| > 1$).*

### 21. Solve:
$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2x - y + 1}{6x - 5y + 4}$$

**Solution:**
Given:
$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2x - y + 1}{6x - 5y + 4}$$

Here $a_1 = 2, b_1 = -1$ and $a_2 = 6, b_2 = -5$.
Notice that $\frac{a_1}{a_2} = \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3} \ne \frac{b_1}{b_2} = \frac{-1}{-5} = \frac{1}{5}$.

**Step 1: Transformation to homogeneous form**
Let $x = X + h$ and $y = Y + k$ where $h, k$ are constants, so $dx = dX, dy = dY$:
$$\frac{dY}{dX} = \frac{2(X + h) - (Y + k) + 1}{6(X + h) - 5(Y + k) + 4} = \frac{2X - Y + (2h - k + 1)}{6X - 5Y + (6h - 5k + 4)}$$

Choose $h$ and $k$ to satisfy:
1. $2h - k + 1 = 0 \implies k = 2h + 1$
2. $6h - 5k + 4 = 0$

Substitute $k$ into (2):
$$6h - 5(2h + 1) + 4 = 0 \implies 6h - 10h - 5 + 4 = 0 \implies -4h - 1 = 0 \implies h = -\frac{1}{4}$$
$$k = 2\left(-\frac{1}{4}\right) + 1 = -\frac{1}{2} + 1 = \frac{1}{2}$$

Thus $X = x + \frac{1}{4}$ and $Y = y - \frac{1}{2}$. The equation reduces to homogeneous form:
$$\frac{dY}{dX} = \frac{2X - Y}{6X - 5Y}$$

**Step 2: Substitution $Y = vX$**
Then $\frac{dY}{dX} = v + X \frac{dv}{dX}$:
$$v + X \frac{dv}{dX} = \frac{2X - vX}{6X - 5vX} = \frac{2 - v}{6 - 5v}$$
$$X \frac{dv}{dX} = \frac{2 - v}{6 - 5v} - v = \frac{2 - v - 6v + 5v^2}{6 - 5v} = \frac{5v^2 - 7v + 2}{6 - 5v}$$

**Step 3: Separate variables**
$$\frac{6 - 5v}{5v^2 - 7v + 2} \, dv = \frac{dX}{X}$$

Factorizing the denominator:
$$5v^2 - 7v + 2 = 5v^2 - 5v - 2v + 2 = (5v - 2)(v - 1)$$

Resolving into partial fractions:
$$\frac{6 - 5v}{(5v - 2)(v - 1)} = \frac{A}{5v - 2} + \frac{B}{v - 1}$$
$$6 - 5v = A(v - 1) + B(5v - 2)$$
- Put $v = 1$: $6 - 5 = B(5 - 2) \implies 1 = 3B \implies B = \frac{1}{3}$
- Put $v = \frac{2}{5}$: $6 - 2 = A\left(\frac{2}{5} - 1\right) \implies 4 = A\left(-\frac{3}{5}\right) \implies A = -\frac{20}{3}$

Integrating both sides:
$$-\frac{20}{3} \int \frac{dv}{5v - 2} + \frac{1}{3} \int \frac{dv}{v - 1} = \int \frac{dX}{X} + C_0$$
$$-\frac{20}{3} \cdot \frac{\ln|5v - 2|}{5} + \frac{1}{3} \ln|v - 1| = \ln|X| + C_0$$
$$-\frac{4}{3} \ln|5v - 2| + \frac{1}{3} \ln|v - 1| = \ln|X| + C_0$$

Multiply by 3:
$$\ln|v - 1| - 4\ln|5v - 2| = 3\ln|X| + 3C_0 = \ln|X^3| + \ln C_1$$
$$\ln\left| \frac{v - 1}{(5v - 2)^4} \right| = \ln|C_1 X^3| \implies \frac{v - 1}{(5v - 2)^4} = C_1 X^3$$

**Step 4: Substitute back $v = \frac{Y}{X}$**
$$\frac{\frac{Y}{X} - 1}{\left( \frac{5Y - 2X}{X} \right)^4} = \frac{(Y - X) X^3}{(5Y - 2X)^4} = C_1 X^3 \implies \frac{Y - X}{(5Y - 2X)^4} = C_1$$
$$Y - X = C_1 (5Y - 2X)^4$$

**Step 5: Substitute back $X = x + \frac{1}{4}$ and $Y = y - \frac{1}{2}$**
- $Y - X = \left(y - \frac{1}{2}\right) - \left(x + \frac{1}{4}\right) = y - x - \frac{3}{4} = \frac{4(y - x) - 3}{4}$
- $5Y - 2X = 5\left(y - \frac{1}{2}\right) - 2\left(x + \frac{1}{4}\right) = 5y - 2x - \frac{5}{2} - \frac{1}{2} = 5y - 2x - 3$

Thus:
$$\frac{4(y - x) - 3}{4} = C_1 (5y - 2x - 3)^4$$
$$4(y - x) - 3 = C (5y - 2x - 3)^4$$

where $C = 4C_1$ is an arbitrary constant.

### 22. Find the general solution of the differential equation:
$$\frac{d^2 y}{dx^2} + 2 \frac{dy}{dx} + y = 2x + x^2$$

**Solution:**
In operator notation with $D = \frac{d}{dx}$:
$$(D^2 + 2D + 1)y = x^2 + 2x \implies (D + 1)^2 y = x^2 + 2x$$

**Step 1: Complementary Function ($y_c$):**
The auxiliary equation is:
$$m^2 + 2m + 1 = 0 \implies (m + 1)^2 = 0 \implies m = -1, -1 \quad \text{(repeated root)}$$

$$y_c = (c_1 + c_2 x) e^{-x}$$

**Step 2: Particular Integral ($y_p$):**
$$y_p = \frac{1}{(1 + D)^2} (x^2 + 2x) = (1 + D)^{-2} (x^2 + 2x)$$

Expanding $(1 + D)^{-2}$ in powers of $D$ up to order 2 using binomial expansion:
$$(1 + D)^{-2} = 1 - 2D + 3D^2 - 4D^3 + \dots$$

Applying the operator to $x^2 + 2x$:
1. $1 \cdot (x^2 + 2x) = x^2 + 2x$
2. $-2D(x^2 + 2x) = -2(2x + 2) = -4x - 4$
3. $3D^2(x^2 + 2x) = 3(2) = 6$
4. Higher derivatives are zero ($D^3(x^2 + 2x) = 0$).

Adding these:
$$y_p = (x^2 + 2x) - (4x + 4) + 6 = x^2 - 2x + 2$$

**Step 3: General Solution:**
$$y = y_c + y_p = (c_1 + c_2 x) e^{-x} + x^2 - 2x + 2$$

where $c_1$ and $c_2$ are arbitrary constants.

### 23. Find the Fourier sine series for $f(x) = x^2$ in the interval $0 < x < 3$.

**Solution:**
The Fourier sine series of $f(x)$ on the interval $(0, L)$ with $L = 3$ is:
$$f(x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{3}\right)$$

where the coefficient $b_n$ is:
$$b_n = \frac{2}{L} \int_0^L f(x) \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) dx = \frac{2}{3} \int_0^3 x^2 \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{3}\right) dx$$

**Evaluating the Integral using Generalized Integration by Parts (Bernoulli's formula):**
$$\int u v \, dx = u v_1 - u' v_2 + u'' v_3 - \dots$$
Let $u = x^2 \implies u' = 2x, \quad u'' = 2, \quad u''' = 0$.
Let $v = \sin(kx)$ with $k = \frac{n\pi}{3}$:
$$v_1 = -\frac{\cos kx}{k}, \quad v_2 = -\frac{\sin kx}{k^2}, \quad v_3 = \frac{\cos kx}{k^3}$$

$$\int_0^3 x^2 \sin(kx) \, dx = \left[ -x^2 \frac{\cos kx}{k} + 2x \frac{\sin kx}{k^2} + 2 \frac{\cos kx}{k^3} \right]_0^3$$

Evaluate at upper limit $x = 3$ (where $k \cdot 3 = n\pi$):
- $\cos(n\pi) = (-1)^n$
- $\sin(n\pi) = 0$
$$\text{Upper Limit} = -(3)^2 \frac{(-1)^n}{k} + 0 + 2 \frac{(-1)^n}{k^3} = -\frac{9(-1)^n}{k} + \frac{2(-1)^n}{k^3}$$

Evaluate at lower limit $x = 0$:
$$\text{Lower Limit} = 0 + 0 + 2 \frac{\cos(0)}{k^3} = \frac{2}{k^3}$$

Difference:
$$\int_0^3 x^2 \sin(kx) \, dx = -\frac{9(-1)^n}{k} + \frac{2[(-1)^n - 1]}{k^3}$$

Substitute $k = \frac{n\pi}{3}$:
- $\frac{9}{k} = \frac{9 \cdot 3}{n\pi} = \frac{27}{n\pi}$
- $\frac{2}{k^3} = \frac{2 \cdot 27}{n^3 \pi^3} = \frac{54}{n^3 \pi^3}$

Multiply by $\frac{2}{3}$:
$$b_n = \frac{2}{3} \left[ -\frac{27(-1)^n}{n\pi} + \frac{54((-1)^n - 1)}{n^3 \pi^3} \right] = -\frac{18(-1)^n}{n\pi} + \frac{36((-1)^n - 1)}{n^3 \pi^3}$$
$$b_n = \frac{18(-1)^{n+1}}{n\pi} - \frac{36(1 - (-1)^n)}{n^3 \pi^3}$$

- For even $n = 2m$: $(-1)^n = 1 \implies b_{2m} = -\frac{18}{2m\pi} = -\frac{9}{m\pi}$
- For odd $n = 2m - 1$: $(-1)^n = -1 \implies b_n = \frac{18}{n\pi} - \frac{72}{n^3 \pi^3}$

Thus, the Fourier sine series is:
$$f(x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \left[ \frac{18(-1)^{n+1}}{n\pi} - \frac{36(1 - (-1)^n)}{n^3 \pi^3} \right] \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{3}\right)$$

### 24. Find an analytic function $f(z)$ whose real part is $e^x (x \cos y - y \sin y)$.

**Solution:**
Given real part:
$$u(x, y) = e^x (x \cos y - y \sin y)$$

We use the **Milne-Thomson Method** to construct $f(z)$:
$$f'(z) = u_x(z, 0) - i u_y(z, 0)$$

**Step 1: Compute partial derivatives of $u$:**
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(e^x) \cdot (x \cos y - y \sin y) + e^x \cdot \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x \cos y - y \sin y)$$
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = e^x (x \cos y - y \sin y) + e^x (\cos y) = e^x [(x + 1)\cos y - y \sin y]$$

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = e^x [-x \sin y - (1 \cdot \sin y + y \cos y)] = -e^x [(x + 1)\sin y + y \cos y]$$

**Step 2: Put $x = z$ and $y = 0$:**
Since $\cos 0 = 1$ and $\sin 0 = 0$:
$$u_x(z, 0) = e^z [(z + 1)(1) - 0] = (z + 1) e^z$$
$$u_y(z, 0) = -e^z [(z + 1)(0) + 0] = 0$$

**Step 3: Integrate $f'(z)$:**
$$f'(z) = u_x(z, 0) - i u_y(z, 0) = (z + 1) e^z - i(0) = (z + 1) e^z$$

Integrating with respect to $z$:
$$f(z) = \int (z + 1) e^z \, dz + C$$
$$f(z) = \int z e^z \, dz + \int e^z \, dz + C$$

Using integration by parts on $\int z e^z \, dz = z e^z - e^z$:
$$f(z) = (z e^z - e^z) + e^z + C = z e^z + C$$

where $C$ is an arbitrary complex constant.

### 25. Find the residue of:
$$f(z) = \frac{z}{(z - 1)(z - 2)^2} \quad \text{at its poles.}$$

**Solution:**
Given:
$$f(z) = \frac{z}{(z - 1)(z - 2)^2}$$

The poles of $f(z)$ are given by the roots of the denominator:
$$(z - 1)(z - 2)^2 = 0$$
1. $z = 1$ is a **simple pole** (pole of order 1).
2. $z = 2$ is a **pole of order 2** (double pole).

---

#### 1. Residue at simple pole $z = 1$:
For a simple pole at $z = z_0$:
$$\text{Res}(f, 1) = \lim_{z \to 1} (z - 1) f(z) = \lim_{z \to 1} (z - 1) \cdot \frac{z}{(z - 1)(z - 2)^2}$$
$$\text{Res}(f, 1) = \lim_{z \to 1} \frac{z}{(z - 2)^2} = \frac{1}{(1 - 2)^2} = \frac{1}{(-1)^2} = 1$$

---

#### 2. Residue at pole of order 2 at $z = 2$:
For a pole of order $m$ at $z = z_0$:
$$\text{Res}(f, z_0) = \frac{1}{(m - 1)!} \lim_{z \to z_0} \frac{d^{m-1}}{dz^{m-1}} \left[ (z - z_0)^m f(z) \right]$$

Here $m = 2$ and $z_0 = 2$:
$$\text{Res}(f, 2) = \frac{1}{(2 - 1)!} \lim_{z \to 2} \frac{d}{dz} \left[ (z - 2)^2 \cdot \frac{z}{(z - 1)(z - 2)^2} \right]$$
$$\text{Res}(f, 2) = \lim_{z \to 2} \frac{d}{dz} \left( \frac{z}{z - 1} \right)$$

Evaluating the derivative using the quotient rule:
$$\frac{d}{dz} \left( \frac{z}{z - 1} \right) = \frac{(1)(z - 1) - z(1)}{(z - 1)^2} = \frac{z - 1 - z}{(z - 1)^2} = \frac{-1}{(z - 1)^2}$$

Taking the limit as $z \to 2$:
$$\text{Res}(f, 2) = \lim_{z \to 2} \frac{-1}{(z - 1)^2} = \frac{-1}{(2 - 1)^2} = \frac{-1}{1} = -1$$

---

#### Summary of Residues:
- At $z = 1$: $\text{Res}(f, 1) = 1$
- At $z = 2$: $\text{Res}(f, 2) = -1$

*(Verification: The sum of residues is $\sum \text{Res} = 1 + (-1) = 0$. Since the degree of the denominator exceeds that of the numerator by 2, the residue at infinity $\text{Res}(f, \infty) = 0$, confirming the calculations are exact).*
